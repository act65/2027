From densities to waves: one recipe for the geometries of flow matching

Flow matching quietly assumes your data live in Euclidean space, but when each data point is itself a distribution the straight line and the L2 norm need replacing, and Wasserstein, the linear mixture and Fisher-Rao are the usual choices. We show that all three fall out of one recipe, a rule for how probability mass may change plus a price for each change, and watch the same two bumps slide or cross-fade under each. Run on complex amplitudes, the same recipe gives a fourth geometry, Fubini-Study, the only one with which a neural network can give a density zeros that survive its own small errors.

Flow matching is Euclidean flow matching

Flow matching trains a vector field \(v_\theta\) to transform a source distribution to a data distribution. In its standard form it draws a source point \(x_0\), a data point \(x_1\) and a time \(t \sim U[0,1]\), joins the two points by a straight line, and regresses \(v_\theta\) onto the line’s velocity:

\[\mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\, \parallel v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \parallel^2,\] \[x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1 .\]

Two choices in this loss are Euclidean: the interpolant is a chord, the straight line through the ambient space, and the error is measured in the \(L^2\) norm. Riemannian flow matching keeps the form of the loss and replaces both on a manifold with metric \(g\). The chord becomes the geodesic, and the \(L^2\) norm becomes the metric at the current point:

\[\mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\, \parallel v_\theta(x_t, t) - \dot x_t \parallel^2_{g(x_t)},\] \[x_t = \exp_{x_0}\!\big(t \log_{x_0} x_1\big), \qquad \dot x_t = \frac{\log_{x_t} x_1}{1-t}.\]

Here \(\log_x y\) is the initial velocity of the geodesic from \(x\) to \(y\), scaled so that its length is their distance, and \(\exp_x\) walks along the geodesic with a given initial velocity. These are the exponential and logarithm maps of the manifold, not the elementwise functions: in Euclidean space \(\exp_x(v) = x + v\) and \(\log_x y = y - x\), which gives back the first loss. A geometry enters the loss through two objects: the interpolant \(x_t\) and the norm \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_{g}\). They are not equally important. Because \(g\) depends only on \(x_t\), the best \(v_\theta\) is \(\mathbb E[\dot x_t \mid x_t]\) whatever the norm, so the norm only reweights the regression. The interpolant decides what is learned.

Flow matching between distributions

In this post every data point \(x\) is itself a distribution \(\mu\). For categorical data with \(K\) classes, \(\mu\) is a vector of \(K\) probabilities, the source can be a uniformly random such vector, and \(v_\theta\) outputs a change \(\delta\mu\) whose entries sum to zero. In Fisher and Dirichlet flow matching the data are one-hot vectors, the corners of the simplex, and only the path between them passes through general distributions; statistical-manifold and Wasserstein flow matching also take data points that are themselves general distributions. For a parametric family, such as the Gaussians, \(\mu\) is named by its parameters. For the pictures below, \(\mu\) is a density on the line, stored on a grid. In every case the flow runs on a space of distributions: each point of it is a whole density, the source is a distribution over that space, and the geodesic in the loss is a path of densities.

The setting. Left: a space of distributions, here the Gaussians \(\mathcal N(m, s^2)\), drawn as the half-plane of their parameters; each dot is one whole distribution. The flow carries a draw from the source (grey) to a data point (orange) along the geodesic of the chosen metric (solid; here the exact Fisher–Rao geodesic), not along the straight chord of Euclidean flow matching (dashed). The network \(v_\theta\) predicts the velocity at every point of the path. Right: the three marked points, drawn as the densities they are.

Existing work fills in the metric \(g\) in two ways. Moving mass gives the Wasserstein geometry, used by Wasserstein Flow Matching. Creating and destroying mass in place gives the geometries of simplex flow matching: the flat geometry of linear mixtures, the usual baseline, and the Fisher–Rao geometry of Fisher Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching. All of them come from one recipe:

Fix a rule for how probability mass may change, and a price for changing it. The metric is the cheapest price of a given change. Flow matching then uses the metric’s geodesics as interpolants and its norm in the loss.

The next sections derive each geometry from the recipe on one running example. After that we ask what the recipe gives when it acts on something other than a density.

A running example

Take two Gaussian densities of width \(\sigma\) a distance \(d\) apart, \(\mu_0 = \mathcal N(-d/2, \sigma^2)\) and \(\mu_1 = \mathcal N(d/2, \sigma^2)\), with \(d = 5\sigma\). For each geometry we ask what the geodesic from \(\mu_0\) to \(\mu_1\) does and how long it is. Here are the answers for the three density geometries, before any derivation:

Three geodesics between the same two Gaussians (\(\sigma = 0.7\), \(d = 3.5\)). Press play. The Wasserstein bump slides. The mixture and Fisher–Rao bumps cross-fade without moving, with different timing. Switch "show" to the regression target to see what a flow matching network is trained to output at the current \(t\): the change of density \(\partial_t\mu_t\) produced by \(\dot x_t\). Transport gives a dipole that follows the bump; birth and death give a loss on the left and a gain on the right. Each row is derived in the next three sections. The other endpoint choices reappear with a fourth row later on.

Transport gives Wasserstein

Rule. Mass moves with a velocity field \(v\) and is conserved locally:

\[\partial_t \mu + \nabla\cdot(\mu v) = 0 .\]

This is the continuity (transport) equation, the same equation that links the probability path \(p_t\) of any flow matching model to its vector field \(u_t\). Here it acts one level up. The density \(\mu\) is a single data point, a function of position \(x\) on the line, and \(v(x)\) says where its mass moves; the equation describes one step along the path between two data points.

Cheapest price. Write \(\delta\mu\) for an infinitesimal change of \(\mu\), a tangent vector. Many fields produce the same \(\delta\mu\): adding any \(w\) with \(\nabla\cdot(\mu w) = 0\), a flow that circulates without piling up mass, changes nothing. Charge kinetic energy, \(\int \lVert v\rVert^2\, d\mu\). A circulating part costs energy and moves nothing, so the cheapest field has none: it is a gradient, \(v = \nabla\xi\). Its price is Otto’s metric

\[g^{W}_\mu(\delta\mu,\delta\mu) = \int \lVert\nabla\xi\rVert^2\, d\mu,\] \[\delta\mu = -\nabla\cdot(\mu\nabla\xi).\]

Geodesics. The length of the shortest path is the \(W_2\) distance. Along it every particle moves in a straight line at constant speed (displacement interpolation). This is the interpolant in the loss. It is closed form in one dimension, where it interpolates the quantile functions. Wasserstein Flow Matching uses the closed form between Gaussians and entropic estimates between point clouds.

Running example. The Gaussian slides from \(-d/2\) to \(d/2\) without changing shape, and \(W_2 = d\). The distance grows without bound as the bumps separate.

Birth and death give the mixture

Rule. Mass may not move. It may only appear or disappear in place, at a rate \(r(x)\):

\[\partial_t \mu = r\,\mu, \qquad \int r\, d\mu = 0 ,\]

where the zero-mean condition keeps the total mass at one. There is nothing to choose: \(r = \delta\mu/\mu\). What is left to decide is the price.

Flat price. Charge the change itself, \(\int (r\mu)^2\,dx\). The metric is

\[g^{L^2}_\mu(\delta\mu,\delta\mu) = \int (\delta\mu)^2\, dx ,\]

the same at every \(\mu\), so the geometry is flat.

Geodesics. Straight lines, \(\mu_t = (1-t)\,\mu_0 + t\,\mu_1\), the mixture path. A mixture of densities is a density, so the path never leaves the simplex. In the loss, this is Euclidean flow matching on the probability vector: the interpolant is linear, the target is \(\mu_1 - \mu_0\) at every \(t\), and the norm is Euclidean. It is the linear baseline that Dirichlet Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching compare against. Discrete Flow Matching uses paths of the same mixture form one level up, for the distribution over tokens.

Running example. The left bump fades out at a constant rate while the right bump fades in, and \(\lVert\mu_1 - \mu_0\rVert_{L^2} = 0.90\). That number depends on the units of \(x\): stretching \(x\) by a factor \(c\) divides it by \(\sqrt c\). The flat price is not intrinsic to the distributions.

Fisher’s price gives Fisher-Rao

Same rule, other price. Keep birth and death, and charge the rate instead of the change: the variance of the rate, \(\int r^2\,d\mu\) (its mean is zero). This gives the Fisher–Rao metric,

\[g^{FR}_\mu(\delta\mu,\delta\mu) = \int r^2\, d\mu = \int \frac{(\delta\mu)^2}{\mu}\, dx .\]

Compared with the flat price, it divides by \(\mu\): changing mass that is nearly zero is expensive. Up to scale, it is the only metric on densities that does not depend on how the underlying space is parameterised, which is exactly what the flat price lacked.

The square-root map. Put \(s = \sqrt{\mu}\). Then \(\delta s = \delta\mu/(2\sqrt{\mu})\), so

\[\lVert\delta s\rVert^2_{L^2} = \int \frac{(\delta\mu)^2}{4\mu}\,dx = \tfrac14\, g^{FR}(\delta\mu,\delta\mu).\]

This is why Fisher Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching replace \(p\) by \(\sqrt p\) before anything else. In the coordinates \(s = \sqrt\mu\), Fisher–Rao is the round metric of the unit sphere in \(L^2\), up to the factor \(\tfrac14\). Densities are nonnegative, so they fill only the nonnegative orthant of that sphere. The mixture is flat in \(\mu\); Fisher–Rao is round in \(\sqrt\mu\).

Geodesics. Great circles on the sphere:

\[s_t = \frac{\sin((1-t)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\,s_0 + \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\,s_1,\] \[\cos\Omega = \mathrm{BC} = \int\sqrt{\mu_0\mu_1}\,dx,\] \[d_{FR}(\mu_0,\mu_1) = 2\Omega ,\]

where BC is the Bhattacharyya coefficient. In the loss, the interpolant is this spherical interpolation of \(\sqrt\mu\), and the norm is the \(L^2\) norm of \(\delta s\). Compared with the mixture it changes the timing: because small masses are expensive, the path slows down near the edges of the simplex.

Distributions on three categories. Left: the simplex is a triangle, and the mixture path is a straight line. Right: the square-root map sends the triangle onto an eighth of the unit sphere, seen here from the direction \((1,1,1)\), and the Fisher–Rao path is a great circle. Each path is straight in its own coordinates and curved in the other's. Drag the endpoints. The unit balls show the price of a step. Under the flat price they are equal circles in \(\mu\). Under Fisher–Rao they shrink and flatten towards the edges, where changing a small mass is expensive, and on the sphere they become equal round discs, foreshortened near the rim by the viewing angle. Each edge of the triangle is the two-category simplex, which reappears as the Bloch sphere below.

Running example. \(\mathrm{BC} = e^{-d^2/8\sigma^2}\), which is \(0.04\) at \(d = 5\sigma\). As with the mixture, mass does not slide: the left bump fades out while the right fades in. The distance is \(d_{FR} = 3.05\), close to its maximum of \(\pi\), which every pair of non-overlapping densities reaches. Fisher–Rao barely distinguishes bumps \(5\sigma\) apart from bumps \(50\sigma\) apart. The mixture and Fisher–Rao rows of the opening widget look alike; the difference is in the timing. Here \(\Omega \approx \pi/2\), so the left bump keeps mass about \(\cos^2(\pi t/2)\) rather than \(1-t\). In the regression-target view, the change of density in the Fisher–Rao target shrinks to zero at both ends of the path, while the mixture’s stays the same at every \(t\). The Fisher–Rao path still moves at constant Fisher–Rao speed there; small changes of small masses are what it charges most for.

Same reach, different price. None of these rules restricts where a positive density can go. Birth and death produce any change of total mass zero (\(r = \delta\mu/\mu\)), and so does transport (solve \(\delta\mu = -\nabla\cdot(\mu\nabla\xi)\) for \(\xi\)). The rules and prices differ only in how a change is paid for, and therefore in which path is cheapest. The mixture and Fisher–Rao make the point most sharply: one rule, two prices, two geometries.

An amplitude gives Fubini-Study

So far the recipe has reproduced the geometries that existing flow matching methods use. What if it acts on something other than a density? Every rule so far moves a density, and a density is nonnegative, so all three geometries live in the nonnegative orthant of the square-root sphere. Some data do not. A short-time Fourier transform frame, an MRI measurement or an eigenfunction that changes sign is a signed or complex amplitude, and its sign or phase carries information. To model such data, change the state.

Five quantum words, for ML readers
  • Amplitude ψ: a complex function with ∫|ψ|² = 1. Its density is μ = |ψ|².
  • Global phase: multiplying ψ by a constant eiα. It changes nothing observable.
  • Ray: an amplitude up to its global phase, that is, the set of all eiαψ. Quotienting by this circle of phases is "modulo U(1)".
  • Hamiltonian H: a self-adjoint operator that generates the motion, ∂tψ = −iHψ. Here it is only a control, like v and r above; no physics is assumed.
  • Fidelity F = |⟨ψ₀, ψ₁⟩|: the overlap of two rays, the analogue of BC.

State. A complex amplitude \(\psi\) with density \(\mu = \lvert\psi\rvert^2\). Every density is reached (take \(\psi = \sqrt{\mu}\)), but many amplitudes give the same density: \(e^{i\chi(x)}\psi\) does, for any real function \(\chi\).

Rule. Conservation of mass becomes conservation of the norm, \(\int\lvert\psi\rvert^2 = 1\). A linear evolution \(\partial_t\psi = A\psi\) changes the norm at the rate \(2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi, A\psi\rangle\), which is zero for every \(\psi\) exactly when \(A\) is skew-adjoint. Writing \(A = -iH\) with \(H\) self-adjoint gives

\[\partial_t\psi = -iH\psi, \qquad H^* = H ,\]

the Schrödinger equation, here obtained as the general norm-preserving linear rule. As with transport, no particular \(H\) is chosen, and every motion along the sphere is reachable.

Cheapest price. Charge the control, as for Fisher–Rao: Fisher–Rao charges the variance of the rate \(r\), and here we charge the variance of \(H\) in the state \(\psi\), the energy variance

\[\mathrm{Var}_\psi(H) = \langle H\psi, H\psi\rangle - \langle\psi, H\psi\rangle^2 .\]

It depends on \(H\) only through \(H\psi\), so every \(H\) producing the same motion has the same price, and there is nothing left to minimise. It is also unchanged by \(H \to H + c\) for a constant \(c\), which adds only a global phase. In terms of the motion \(\delta\psi = -iH\psi\), this price is

\[\begin{aligned} g^{FS}_\psi(\delta\psi,\delta\psi) &= \lVert\delta\psi\rVert^2 - \lvert\langle\psi,\delta\psi\rangle\rvert^2 \\ &= \min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}} \lVert\delta\psi + i\beta\psi\rVert^2 , \end{aligned}\]

the Fubini–Study metric. The second form says what it measures. The direction \(i\beta\psi\) only rotates the global phase, so it changes the amplitude but not the ray, and the metric charges a motion only for its component that moves the ray. Wasserstein likewise charges a field only for the part that moves the density. Fisher–Rao charges the variance of the growth rate; Fubini–Study charges the variance of the energy.

Geodesics. Rotate the global phase of \(\psi_1\) so that \(\langle\psi_0,\psi_1\rangle\) is real and nonnegative, and call its value \(F\), the fidelity. The geodesics are great circles, the Fisher–Rao formula applied to amplitudes:

\[\psi_t = \frac{\sin((1-t)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\,\psi_0 + \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\,\psi_1,\] \[\cos\Omega = F = \lvert\langle\psi_0,\psi_1\rangle\rvert, \qquad d_{FS}(\psi_0,\psi_1) = \Omega .\]

The distance is \(\Omega\), not \(2\Omega\) as for Fisher–Rao. That is the factor \(\tfrac14\) in the square-root map: the standard Fisher–Rao distance is arc length on a sphere of radius two, while Fubini–Study uses the unit sphere, on which orthogonal states are \(\pi/2\) apart. To compare the two, halve \(d_{FR}\).

In the loss, the interpolant is this spherical interpolation after the phase alignment, and the norm is the \(L^2\) norm with the \(i\psi\) component removed.

Running example. Give the target a phase, \(\psi_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0}\) and \(\psi_1 = \sqrt{\mu_1}\,e^{ikx}\). The densities are unchanged. The product \(\sqrt{\mu_0\mu_1}\) is \(\mathrm{BC}\) times the \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) density, whose Fourier transform at \(k\) is \(e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2}\), so

\[F = \mathrm{BC}\; e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2} .\]

At \(k = 0\) the geodesic is the Fisher–Rao cross-fade, at distance \(\tfrac12 d_{FR}\). A phase only moves the states apart. At \(d = 5\sigma\) the effect is small, because the bumps barely overlap and are nearly orthogonal already. Move them closer, to \(d = 2\sigma\): then \(\mathrm{BC} = 0.61\) and \(\tfrac12 d_{FR} = 0.92\), and the phase raises \(d_{FS}\) to \(1.34\) at \(k = 2\) and to \(\pi/2\), orthogonal, at \(k = 6\). The densities have not changed.

Fisher-Rao is the part the density sees

The running example suggests that Fubini–Study is Fisher–Rao plus a phase. Write the amplitude in polar form at each point, \(\psi = \sqrt\mu\, e^{i\varphi}\) (Madelung’s coordinates). Then \(\delta\psi = (\delta\sqrt\mu + i\sqrt\mu\,\delta\varphi)\,e^{i\varphi}\), and the two terms are orthogonal at every point:

\[\lVert\delta\psi\rVert^2 = \underbrace{\int (\delta\sqrt\mu)^2\,dx}_{\tfrac14\, g^{FR}(\delta\mu,\delta\mu)} \;+\; \underbrace{\int (\delta\varphi)^2\, d\mu}_{\text{phase}} .\]

Removing the global phase replaces \(\delta\varphi\) by its deviation from its \(\mu\)-weighted mean, so the Fubini–Study metric splits into two orthogonal parts:

\[g^{FS} = \tfrac14\, g^{FR} \;\oplus\; \mathrm{Var}_\mu(\delta\varphi) .\]

A change of modulus costs its Fisher–Rao length; a change of phase costs the variance of the phase change under \(\mu\). This is the infinitesimal form of the result that the Madelung transform is an isometry. Two consequences follow.

  • On nonnegative endpoints the two flows coincide. For \(\psi_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0}\) and \(\psi_1 = \sqrt{\mu_1}\) the inner product is already \(\mathrm{BC} \ge 0\), and the great circle between them has nonnegative coefficients for all \(t\in[0,1]\), so it stays in the orthant. The Fubini–Study geodesic is the Fisher–Rao geodesic, and the sphere used by Fisher Flow is the nonnegative part of this one.
  • A phase mismatch only adds distance. \(F = \lvert\int \overline{\psi_0}\psi_1\rvert \le \int \lvert\psi_0\rvert\lvert\psi_1\rvert = \mathrm{BC}\), with equality exactly when the relative phase is constant where both amplitudes are nonzero. So \(d_{FS} \ge \tfrac12 d_{FR}\). The factor \(e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2}\) in the running example is one instance.

Two points, the Bloch sphere

With two points everything can be drawn. Fix the global phase so that \(\psi = (\cos\tfrac\vartheta2,\ e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\vartheta2)\). The density is \(\mu = (\cos^2\tfrac\vartheta2, \sin^2\tfrac\vartheta2)\). The modulus part of the metric is \(\tfrac14\delta\vartheta^2\), and the phase part is \(\mu_1\mu_2\,\delta\varphi^2 = \tfrac14\sin^2\vartheta\,\delta\varphi^2\), so

\[g^{FS} = \tfrac14\left(\delta\vartheta^2 + \sin^2\vartheta\,\delta\varphi^2\right),\]

the round sphere of radius \(\tfrac12\), known as the Bloch sphere. Height on the sphere is the density (\(\cos\vartheta = \mu_1 - \mu_2\)), and longitude is the relative phase. Nonnegative amplitudes (\(\varphi = 0\)) form a half-meridian from pole to pole: this arc is Fisher–Rao, an edge of the eighth-sphere in the earlier figure. Signed real amplitudes (\(\varphi \in \{0, \pi\}\)) form the full meridian, and complex amplitudes fill the sphere.

Drag to rotate; the sliders θ and φ are the polar angle and the relative phase of each state. "Two nonnegative states" (the default): the geodesic stays on the orange arc, and its length equals half the Fisher–Rao distance. "Same density, other phase": both states sit at the same height, so their densities are equal and the Fisher–Rao distance is zero, while the Fubini–Study distance is not. "Two complex states" is the general case. The pink curve is the path under a constant Hamiltonian whose axis is tilted away from the geodesic's: it also preserves the norm and connects the two states, but it is a small circle and longer. At zero tilt it is the geodesic.

The picture also shows what happens at a density’s boundary. A distribution with all its mass on one point is a corner of the simplex, but on the Bloch sphere it is a pole, an ordinary point. A nonnegative path must end there; a complex path can pass through.

Geodesics, side by side

Squaring the Fubini–Study geodesic gives its density:

\[\mu_t = a_t^2\,\mu_0 + b_t^2\,\mu_1 + 2a_t b_t\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\overline{\psi_0}\,\psi_1\big),\]

where \(a_t, b_t\) are the two sine coefficients. The first two terms are the Fisher–Rao cross-fade. The third is an interference term, which can be negative: where it cancels the other two, the density vanishes. In the running example it is \(2a_tb_t\sqrt{\mu_0\mu_1}\cos(kx)\), a fringe pattern of wavenumber \(k\) where the two bumps overlap. A nonnegative geometry cannot produce it, because positive combinations of nonnegative amplitudes are nonnegative.

The opening figure with its fourth row, here at \(d = 2\sigma = 1.4\) and \(k = 6\) so that the bumps overlap. Set \(k = 0\): the Fisher–Rao and Fubini–Study rows become identical. Raise \(k\): the target density does not change, but fringes appear. In the regression-target view the thin lines are the amplitude velocity \(\dot\psi_t\), the quantity the Fubini–Study loss actually regresses on; it oscillates even where \(\partial_t\mu_t\) is small. The other endpoints: an "oscillator state" is a Gaussian times a cubic polynomial, a real amplitude with three sign changes. The Wasserstein path must open its three zeros by moving mass aside, while the Fubini–Study path makes them by cancellation. The wave packets cos 3x → cos 6x show the same between two signed waves.

The fringes are not mass moving anywhere. A pure phase change moves no density at all, so it costs nothing under Wasserstein or Fisher–Rao, while Fubini–Study charges it.

The phase is invisible to samples

Suppose the data are samples from \(\mu = \lvert\psi\rvert^2\). Their distribution depends on \(\mu\) alone, so every statistic computed from them is unchanged by any phase \(e^{i\chi(x)}\). This covers likelihoods, MMD, sliced Wasserstein distances and classifier-based scores. At its best, a flow that also carries a phase fits such data exactly as well as a flow on \(\mu\), and no better. On nonnegative data the two are the same flow.

The phase becomes measurable as soon as the data include a second basis. In the running example, \(e^{ikx}\) leaves the position density alone but shifts the Fourier transform by \(k\). Physicists call the Fourier variable momentum. In general the Fourier density \(\lvert\hat\psi(p)\rvert^2\) changes with \(\chi\). This is how quantum state tomography recovers a state from measurements in several bases. It is also why recovering a signal from the magnitude of a single Fourier transform is ill-posed, while overlapping short-time transforms usually pin it down.

Samples from a Gaussian amplitude with a phase χ, against samples from the same Gaussian with no phase. The option χ = kx is the running example's phase. In position, the two-sample test separates them only at its false-positive rate, whatever the phase. In momentum (the Fourier variable) it separates them once the phase is strong enough: kx shifts the momentum distribution, and a chirp widens it.

Geometry and representation

So far the fourth column has been a geometry: a metric, and the geodesics it puts in the loss. It also changes a second thing, the representation, meaning the function that the network outputs and the flow carries. The two are separate choices. Fisher Flow Matching already makes one of them. Fisher–Rao in \(p\) and Fisher–Rao in \(\sqrt p\) are the same geometry in different coordinates, and the square root is chosen because the geodesics, and the outputs, are simpler there.

The representation matters most at a node, a point where the density vanishes. Take \(\mu(x) \propto x^2 e^{-x^2}\). The density is smooth and touches zero at the origin. Its nonnegative root \(\sqrt\mu \propto \lvert x\rvert\, e^{-x^2/2}\) has a kink there, while the signed root \(\psi \propto x\, e^{-x^2/2}\) is smooth and has the same density.

A smooth approximator fits the signed root far more easily: with one zero, eight Fourier modes fit \(\psi\) to within 1%, while \(\sqrt\mu\) is still above 1% at forty, because a kink makes the error of a smooth fit fall only like a power of the number of modes. The other common outputs do no better at a node: \(\mu\) itself must stay nonnegative and touch its bound there, and \(\log\mu\), the natural output of a softmax, goes to minus infinity.

Zeros also separate the geometries along a path. Take a source with no zeros and a target with some, follow the geodesic between them, and ask what changes when a network, which outputs smooth functions, has to produce it.

Geodesics from a Gaussian with no zeros to a target with one, two or three; red marks are zeros of \(\mu_t\). Press play. The slider \(\varepsilon\) blurs each geometry's output at scale \(\varepsilon\), a simple stand-in for a network's limited resolution: the transport map for Wasserstein, \(\sqrt\mu\) for Fisher–Rao and \(\psi\) for Fubini–Study. At the default \(\varepsilon = 0.05\), Wasserstein and Fisher–Rao produce dips but no zeros, even at \(t = 1\), while Fubini–Study keeps every zero, made by cancellation: for an odd target one node arrives from far away, and the others form in pairs partway along. Set \(\varepsilon = 0\) for the exact geodesics. Fisher–Rao then reaches its zeros only at \(t = 1\), and Wasserstein has them from the first instant, for the reason given below. The same \(\varepsilon\) is harsher on the map than on the roots, because each bump of the target comes from a narrow range of source points.

The three behaviours follow from the formulas. Along a Fisher–Rao geodesic, \(\sqrt{\mu_t} = a_t\sqrt{\mu_0} + b_t\sqrt{\mu_1}\) with \(a_t, b_t \ge 0\), so the density vanishes only where both endpoints vanish, which a source with no zeros never does. The mixture behaves the same way. Along a Fubini–Study geodesic, \(\psi_t = a_t\psi_0 + b_t\psi_1\) vanishes wherever the two terms cancel. Wasserstein moves mass, and a flow that moves samples acts as a map \(T\). In one dimension the density it produces is

\[\mu\big(T(z)\big) = \frac{\mu_0(z)}{T'(z)} ,\]

so from a source with no zeros the target can vanish only where \(T'(z) = \infty\). The optimal map has exactly that, a vertical tangent at each zero of the target, and the maps \((1-t)\,z + t\,T(z)\) along the geodesic inherit it for every \(t > 0\). So the exact geodesic has each zero from the first instant, as a single point at the centre of a notch. It can do this only because its velocity field is not smooth: the field inherits the infinite slope too. That is a property of the geometry, not of anything a network would output. A flow with a smooth velocity field, which is what flow matching learns, is a diffeomorphism at every time, so from a source with no zeros it never produces one, even at \(t = 1\). It can only approach the target, with dips that deepen as the network sharpens. A smooth \(\sqrt\mu\) meets a milder version of the same limit. It cannot cross zero, only touch it, and a touching root makes \(\mu\) vanish like \(x^4\) instead of the target’s \(x^2\); a blurred root does not reach zero at all. A smooth \(\psi\) has no such limit, because it passes through zero by changing sign. That zero also survives small errors: a small error in \(\psi\) moves it but cannot remove it, whereas a zero made by touching, in \(\mu\) or \(\sqrt\mu\), disappears under the smallest upward error and turns negative under the smallest downward one. A network never outputs anything exactly, so only a sign change gives it zeros it can rely on. In the same way, a continuous change of coordinates cannot change the number of sign changes of the function it transports, the topological mismatch that Waveflow handles with spline priors for fermionic wave functions. Neural networks in physics have represented the signed or complex amplitude directly since Carleo and Troyer.

None of this contradicts the previous section. Samples cannot tell \(\psi\) from \(\lvert\psi\rvert\), so choosing a signed root changes nothing the data can check. It changes what the network has to output. The fourth column therefore offers two things, which are worth keeping apart. Its geometry, the phase direction and the interference term, helps only when the data carry a sign or a phase. Its representation, an amplitude allowed to change sign, can help whenever the densities have zeros. Whether an easier function to output also makes the flow easier to train is an empirical question, and the recipe does not answer it.

Other rules and prices

The recipe has three slots, the state, the rule and the price, and each can be filled in other ways. None of the following is needed for the rest of the post.

  • Both rules at once. Allow transport and birth and death together, and add their prices. The result is the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao, or Hellinger–Kantorovich, geometry of unbalanced optimal transport. A length scale set by the relative price decides when moving mass is cheaper than destroying and recreating it. Its geodesics between general densities have no closed form.
  • Other prices for transport. Weighting the kinetic energy by a nonlinear function of \(\mu\) gives the transport distances of Dolbeault, Nazaret and Savaré. Measuring the velocity field in a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space gives the Stein geometry, under which Stein variational gradient descent is a gradient flow.
  • Jumps on a graph. If the categories have structure (ordinal labels, neighbouring tokens), let mass hop between neighbouring categories along the edges of a graph. Pricing the hops like kinetic energy gives Maas’s discrete Wasserstein distance. Here nearby categories are close, unlike under Fisher–Rao. The rule is a continuous-time Markov chain, the kind of process that discrete flow matching samples, though there its state is a token rather than a distribution.
  • Transport with noise. Add diffusion to the transport rule and keep the kinetic price, and the cheapest path is the Schrödinger bridge of entropic optimal transport. It is a regularised geodesic rather than a metric one.
  • Matrices instead of amplitudes. Fubini–Study quotients an amplitude by one global phase. Quotienting a matrix of amplitudes by a unitary matrix gives the Bures metric on mixed quantum states. On Gaussian covariances this is the Bures–Wasserstein distance, which is \(W_2\) between centred Gaussians, so the fourth column meets the first.
  • The geometric mixture. Information geometry pairs the mixture path, straight in \(\mu\), with the exponential path, straight in \(\log\mu\): \(\mu_t \propto \mu_0^{1-t}\mu_1^{t}\). The second comes from an affine connection, not from a price, so it sits outside the recipe.

Which geometry for which data

The running example already sorts the four.

  • Wasserstein, when distances in the underlying space should matter. Bumps far apart are far apart, and the interpolant moves mass rather than teleporting it. Point clouds and spatial densities are examples.
  • Mixture, as the baseline. It is the simplest and is exact on the simplex, but its price ignores how much mass is already present, and its distance depends on the units of \(x\). Dirichlet Flow Matching reports that linear flow matching on the simplex behaves pathologically and was built to avoid it, and statistical-manifold flow matching benchmarks against it.
  • Fisher–Rao, when the categories are exchangeable. Its distance ignores where mass sits, does not depend on units, and saturates at \(\pi\) once the supports separate. Categorical data are the natural case.
  • Fubini–Study, when the sign or phase is part of the object: quantum states measured in several bases, STFT frames, MRI \(k\)-space, eigenfunctions that change sign. On samples of a density its geometry adds nothing, since no statistic of the samples sees the phase and on nonnegative data it is Fisher–Rao. Its representation is a separate matter: when the densities have zeros, a signed amplitude is the smoother function to output.

The mixture, Fisher–Rao and Fubini–Study geometries have closed-form geodesics, exponential and logarithm maps, and Wasserstein does in one dimension and between Gaussians, so in these cases the Riemannian objective applies as it stands. The recipe identifies the space the data live in. It does not say which path through that space is easiest for a network to learn, which remains an empirical question for each kind of data.

From densities to amplitudes: one recipe for the geometries of flow matching

Flow matching assumes your data lives in Euclidean space, but when each data point is a distribution, the straight line and the L2 norm need replacing, and Wasserstein, the linear mixture and Fisher-Rao are the usual choices. We show that all three fall out of one recipe, a rule for how probability mass may change plus a price for each change, and watch the same two bumps slide or cross-fade under each. Run on complex amplitudes, the same recipe gives a fourth geometry, Fubini-Study; samples cannot see its phase, but a signed amplitude lets a network represent zeros of a density that survive small errors.

Flow matching is Euclidean flow matching

Flow matching trains a vector field \(v_\theta\) to transform a source distribution to a data distribution. In its standard form it draws a source point \(x_0\), a data point \(x_1\) and a time \(t \sim U[0,1]\), joins the two points by a straight line, and regresses \(v_\theta\) onto the line’s velocity:

\[\mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\, \parallel v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \parallel^2,\] \[x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1 .\]

Two choices in this loss are Euclidean: the interpolant is a chord, the straight line through the ambient space, and the error is measured in the Euclidean norm. Riemannian flow matching keeps the form of the loss and replaces both on a manifold with metric \(g\). The chord becomes the geodesic, and the Euclidean norm becomes the metric at the current point:

\[\mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\, \parallel v_\theta(x_t, t) - \dot x_t \parallel^2_{g(x_t)},\] \[x_t = \exp_{x_0}\!\big(t \log_{x_0} x_1\big), \qquad \dot x_t = \frac{\log_{x_t} x_1}{1-t}.\]

Here \(\log_x y\) is the initial velocity of the geodesic from \(x\) to \(y\), scaled so that its length is their distance, and \(\exp_x\) walks along the geodesic with a given initial velocity. These are the exponential and logarithm maps of the manifold, not the elementwise functions: in Euclidean space \(\exp_x(v) = x + v\) and \(\log_x y = y - x\), which gives back the first loss. A geometry enters the loss through two objects: the interpolant \(x_t\) and the norm \(\parallel \cdot\parallel_{g}\). They are not equally important. Because \(g\) depends only on \(x_t\), the best \(v_\theta\) is \(\mathbb E[\dot x_t \mid x_t]\) whatever the norm, so the norm only reweights the regression. The interpolant decides what is learned.

Flow matching between distributions

In this post every data point is itself a distribution \(\mu\): a whole density takes the place that the vector \(x_t\) had above. From here on, \(x\) is no longer a data point but a position, the variable a density is a function of, as in \(\mu(x)\). For categorical data with \(K\) classes, \(\mu\) is a vector of \(K\) probabilities, the source can be a uniformly random such vector, and \(v_\theta\) outputs a velocity \(\dot\mu\) whose entries sum to zero. In Fisher and Dirichlet flow matching the data are one-hot vectors, the corners of the simplex, and only the path between them passes through general distributions; statistical-manifold and Wasserstein flow matching also take data points that are themselves general distributions. For a parametric family, such as the Gaussians, \(\mu\) is named by its parameters. For the pictures below, \(\mu\) is a density on the line, stored on a grid. In every case the flow runs on a space of distributions: each point of it is a whole density, the source is a distribution over that space, and the geodesic in the loss is a path of densities.

In symbols, a data point is a density \(\mu(x)\) over \(x\), a path of data points is a density \(\mu(x,t)\) that changes with \(t\) (also written \(\mu_t(x)\)), and its velocity at time \(t\) is the function

\[\dot\mu(x) = \frac{\partial \mu(x,t)}{\partial t}, \qquad \int \dot\mu(x)\,dx = 0 ,\]

where the integral is zero because the total mass stays one. This \(\dot\mu\) plays the role of \(\dot x_t\) in the loss above, and a metric \(g_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu)\) is a rule that assigns this velocity a squared length, which may depend on where the path is, \(\mu\).

Existing work fills in the metric \(g\) in two ways. Moving mass gives the Wasserstein geometry, used by Wasserstein Flow Matching. Creating and destroying mass in place gives the geometries of simplex flow matching: the flat geometry of linear mixtures, the usual baseline, and the Fisher-Rao geometry of Fisher Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching. All of them come from one recipe, which the next section sets out.

A geometry here is a rule plus a price, and one recipe turns them into all four geometries; moving from densities to amplitudes adds exactly one new direction, a phase, which samples cannot see, so what it offers sample data is a representation rather than a new geometry.

One recipe

Flow matching always needs two things: an interpolant between a source point and a data point, such as the straight line above, a Gaussian conditional path or a stochastic interpolant, and a norm to measure the error of a velocity. Here the two points are densities, \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu_1\), so the interpolant is a path of densities and the velocity is \(\dot\mu\). Many paths join two densities, so we want the shortest one, and “shortest” needs a length for every velocity: a metric \(g_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu)\). Each geometry below gets its metric and geodesic in the same four steps. They act on a state, the thing that changes along the path: for now the density \(\mu\) itself; later, an amplitude. The state, the rule and the price are choices; the cheapest price and the geodesic follow from them.

A rule. Say how probability mass may change. The rule takes a control \(u\), whatever we are allowed to adjust, and turns it into a change of density. Applied for a short time \(\tau\), starting from \(\mu\), it gives a new density

\[\tilde\mu(x) = \mu(x) + \tau\,\dot\mu(x), \qquad \dot\mu = R_\mu(u),\]

where \(R_\mu\) is the rule written as a map from controls to changes. We ask three things of it: it is linear in \(u\) (twice the control gives twice the change), it conserves total mass, \(\int\dot\mu(x)\,dx = 0\), and it keeps \(\tilde\mu\) nonnegative.

A price. Charge for the control, not for the change: a cost \(c_\mu(u)\) for applying \(u\) at \(\mu\). We ask that it is quadratic in \(u\), so that the length it defines comes from an inner product and the loss is a weighted least squares; that it charges \(u\) and \(-u\) alike; and that it is positive unless the control leaves the state unchanged.

The cheapest price. Now ask what it costs to get from \(\mu\) to \(\tilde\mu\). Several controls may lead there, and the squared length of the change is the price of the cheapest one:

\[g_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = \min\big\{\, c_\mu(u) \;:\; R_\mu(u) = \dot\mu \,\big\} .\]

The minimum has work to do only when different controls produce the same change at different prices: transport in two or more dimensions, where many velocity fields move mass the same way. Elsewhere either the change fixes the control (birth and death) or all controls with the same effect cost the same (amplitudes), and the price alone sets the metric.

Geodesics. With a length for every velocity, the geodesic from \(\mu_0\) to \(\mu_1\) is the path whose total price \(\int_0^1 g_{\mu_t}(\dot\mu_t,\dot\mu_t)\,dt\) is smallest; that smallest total is the squared distance between them. For transport this is optimal transport: the cheapest way to move one density onto another.

Fix a rule for how probability mass may change, and a price for changing it. The metric is the cheapest price of a given change. Flow matching then uses the metric’s geodesics as interpolants and its norm in the loss.

None of these geometries is new, and neither is the mathematics behind the recipe. What this post adds is the single frame: the geometries of flow matching on distributions side by side, derived the same way on one example, the recipe run once more on amplitudes, and the line it draws between a geometry and the function a network outputs. The next sections derive each geometry on one running example; after that we ask what the recipe gives when it acts on something other than a density.

A running example

Take two Gaussian densities of width \(\sigma\) a distance \(d\) apart. For three different geometries, we visualise the geodesic from \(\mu_0\) to \(\mu_1\):

Three geodesics between the same two Gaussians. Left: every Gaussian $\mathcal N(m, w^2)$, of mean $m$ and width $w$, is one point of the plane, so each data point of the flow is a point; drag $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$. Right: $\mu_t$ at the current $t$, as a density. The Wasserstein path stays a single Gaussian, sliding along the straight line, because in one dimension $W_2^2$ between Gaussians is $\Delta m^2 + \Delta w^2$. The mixture and Fisher-Rao paths leave the family. Both are mixtures of Gaussians whose components stay where they are while their weights shift: two components for the mixture, and three for Fisher-Rao, whose third component $\nu$ is the normalised $\sqrt{\mu_0(x)\mu_1(x)}$; its weight is at most $\mathrm{BC}(\mu_0,\mu_1) = 0.04$ here.

Transport gives Wasserstein

Rule. Mass moves with a velocity field \(v(x,t)\) and is conserved locally (for \(x \in \mathbb R^n\); \(\nabla\cdot\) is the divergence, the net outflow, and \(\nabla\) the gradient):

\[\frac{\partial \mu(x,t)}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\big(\mu(x,t)\,v(x,t)\big) = 0 .\]

This is the continuity (transport) equation, the same equation that links the probability path of any flow matching model to its vector field. Here it acts one level up. The density \(\mu(x,t)\) is a single data point at time \(t\), a function of position \(x\) on the line, and \(v(x,t)\) says where its mass moves; the equation describes one step along the path between two data points.

Cheapest price. Here, unlike for the other rules, several controls produce the same change, so the cheapest one has to be found. Adding to \(v(x)\) any field \(w(x)\) with \(\nabla\cdot\big(\mu(x)\,w(x)\big) = 0\), a flow that circulates without piling up mass anywhere, moves the mass differently but leaves the change of density \(\dot\mu(x)\) the same. Charge kinetic energy, \(\int \parallel v(x)\parallel^2\,\mu(x)\,dx\), the squared speed of the mass averaged over where the mass is. A circulating part is orthogonal to a gradient field under this price, so it only adds energy and moves nothing; the cheapest field has none, and is a gradient, \(v(x) = \nabla\xi(x)\). The potential \(\xi(x)\) is an ordinary function of \(x\), fixed by \(\mu\) and \(\dot\mu\) through the second equation below. The price is Otto’s metric

\[g^{W}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = \int \parallel\nabla\xi(x)\parallel^2\,\mu(x)\,dx,\] \[-\nabla\cdot\big(\mu(x)\,\nabla\xi(x)\big) = \dot\mu(x).\]

Geodesics. With this metric, the length of the shortest path is the 2-Wasserstein distance \(W_2(\mu_0,\mu_1)\). Along it every particle moves in a straight line at constant speed (displacement interpolation). This is the interpolant in the loss. It is closed form in one dimension, where it interpolates the quantile functions. Wasserstein Flow Matching uses the closed form between Gaussians and entropic estimates between point clouds.

Birth and death give the mixture

Rule. Mass may not move. It may only appear or disappear in place, at a rate \(r(x,t)\):

\[\frac{\partial \mu(x,t)}{\partial t} = r(x,t)\,\mu(x,t),\] \[\int r(x,t)\,\mu(x,t)\,dx = 0 ,\]

where the zero-mean condition keeps the total mass at one. Unlike transport, there is no cheapest control to find: the change fixes the rate, \(r(x) = \dot\mu(x)/\mu(x)\), so the price alone sets the metric.

Flat price. Charge the change itself, \(\int \big(r(x)\,\mu(x)\big)^2\,dx\). The metric is

\[g^{L^2}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = \int \dot\mu(x)^2\, dx ,\]

the same at every \(\mu\), so the geometry is flat.

Geodesics. Straight lines, \(\mu_t(x) = (1-t)\,\mu_0(x) + t\,\mu_1(x)\), the mixture path. A mixture of densities is a density, so the path never leaves the simplex. In the loss, this is Euclidean flow matching on the probability vector: the interpolant is linear, the target is \(\mu_1 - \mu_0\) at every \(t\), and the norm is Euclidean. It is the linear baseline that Dirichlet Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching compare against.

Fisher’s price gives Fisher-Rao

Same rule, other price. Keep birth and death, and charge the rate instead of the change: the variance of the rate, \(\int r(x)^2\,\mu(x)\,dx\). This gives the Fisher-Rao metric,

\[g^{FR}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = \int \frac{\dot\mu(x)^2}{\mu(x)}\, dx .\]

Compared with the flat price it divides by \(\mu\), so changing mass that is nearly zero is expensive. It is also the curvature of the KL divergence: for a small step, \(\mathrm{KL}\big(\mu \,\|\, \mu + \tau\dot\mu\big) = \tfrac12\tau^2\, g^{FR}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) + O(\tau^3)\). Up to scale, it is the only metric that is unchanged when categories are merged or split, which is Chentsov’s theorem. For densities on a closed space of two or more dimensions it is also the only one unchanged by any smooth relabelling of \(x\); our pictures, on a line, fall outside that result. This invariance is what the flat price lacked.

The square root. In the coordinates \(s(x) = \sqrt{\mu(x)}\) the velocity is \(\dot s(x) = \dot\mu(x)/\big(2\sqrt{\mu(x)}\big)\), so

\[g^{FR}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = 4\int \dot s(x)^2\,dx .\]

Since \(\int s(x)^2\,dx = \int\mu(x)\,dx = 1\), the square roots lie on the unit sphere, and Fisher-Rao is four times its round metric: densities fill the sphere’s nonnegative part. This is why Fisher Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching replace \(\mu\) by \(\sqrt\mu\) before anything else. The mixture is flat in \(\mu\); Fisher-Rao is round in \(\sqrt\mu\).

Geodesics. Great circles on that sphere: the interpolant in the loss is the spherical interpolation (slerp) of \(\sqrt{\mu_0}\) and \(\sqrt{\mu_1}\) through the angle \(\Omega\) between them,

\[\sqrt{\mu_t(x)} = a_t\sqrt{\mu_0(x)} + b_t\sqrt{\mu_1(x)},\] \[a_t = \frac{\sin((1-t)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega},\qquad b_t = \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}.\]

Its cosine is the overlap of the two square roots, \(\cos\Omega = \mathrm{BC}(\mu_0,\mu_1) = \int\sqrt{\mu_0(x)\,\mu_1(x)}\,dx\), known as the Bhattacharyya coefficient: it is \(1\) when the densities are equal and \(0\) when they do not overlap. The distance is \(d_{FR}(\mu_0,\mu_1) = 2\Omega\), twice the angle because of the factor 4 in the metric. Because small masses are expensive, the path slows down near the edges of the simplex.

Distributions on three categories. Left: the simplex is a triangle, and the mixture path is a straight line. Right: after the square-root map, each distribution is the point $(\sqrt{\mu_1}, \sqrt{\mu_2}, \sqrt{\mu_3})$, whose squared coordinates sum to one, so it lies on the unit sphere; the coordinates are nonnegative, so the distributions fill the part of the sphere with all three coordinates positive, one eighth of it, seen here from the direction $(1,1,1)$. The Fisher-Rao path is a great circle. Each path is straight in its own coordinates and curved in the other's. Drag the endpoints. The unit balls show the price of a step. Under the flat price they are equal circles in $\mu$. Under Fisher-Rao they shrink and flatten towards the edges, where changing a small mass is expensive, and on the sphere they become equal round discs, foreshortened near the rim by the viewing angle. Each edge of the triangle is the two-category simplex, which reappears as a half-meridian of the Bloch sphere below.

Same reach, different price. None of these rules restricts where a positive density can go. Birth and death produce any change of total mass zero (\(r(x) = \dot\mu(x)/\mu(x)\)), and so does transport (solve \(-\nabla\cdot\big(\mu(x)\nabla\xi(x)\big) = \dot\mu(x)\) for \(\xi\)). The rules and prices differ only in how a change is paid for, and therefore in which path is cheapest. The mixture and Fisher-Rao make the point most sharply: one rule, two prices, two geometries.

An amplitude gives Fubini-Study

So far the recipe has reproduced the geometries that existing flow matching methods use. What if it acts on something other than a density? Every rule so far moves a density, and a density is nonnegative, so all three geometries live in the nonnegative orthant of the square-root sphere. Some data do not. A short-time Fourier transform frame, an MRI measurement or an eigenfunction that changes sign is a signed or complex amplitude, and its sign or phase carries information. Normalising such an amplitude keeps its shape; its overall scale, a loudness or an intensity, is a separate number to model alongside it. To model such data, change the state.

Five quantum words, for ML readers
  • Amplitude $\psi(x)$: a complex function with $\int |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1$. Its density is $\mu(x) = |\psi(x)|^2$.
  • Global phase: multiplying $\psi$ by a constant $e^{i\alpha}$. It changes neither the density nor anything used below.
  • Ray: an amplitude up to its global phase, that is, the set of all $e^{i\alpha}\psi$. Quotienting by this circle of phases is "modulo $U(1)$".
  • Hamiltonian $H$: a self-adjoint operator that generates the motion, $\frac{\partial \psi(x,t)}{\partial t} = -iH\psi(x,t)$. Here it is only a control, like $v$ and $r$ above; no physics is assumed.
  • Fidelity $F(\psi_0,\psi_1) = |\langle\psi_0, \psi_1\rangle |$: the overlap of two rays, the analogue of BC.

State. A complex amplitude \(\psi(x)\) with density \(\mu(x) = \lvert\psi(x)\rvert^2\). Every density is reached (\(\psi(x) = \sqrt{\mu(x)}\)), but many amplitudes give the same density: \(e^{i\chi(x)}\psi(x)\) does, for any real function \(\chi(x)\), a change of the phase at each point. A constant phase, \(e^{i\alpha}\psi(x)\), changes nothing used below, so the state is the amplitude up to this global phase, called a ray and written \([\psi]\).

Rule. Conservation of mass becomes conservation of the norm, \(\int\lvert\psi(x,t)\rvert^2\,dx = 1\). The linear evolutions that preserve it are \(\frac{\partial \psi(x,t)}{\partial t} = -iH\psi(x,t)\) with \(H\) self-adjoint (the analogue of a symmetric matrix): the Schrödinger equation, used here only as the general norm-preserving rule, with \(H\) as the control.

Price. Use the inner product \(\langle\psi,\phi\rangle = \int \overline{\psi(x)}\,\phi(x)\,dx\), where the bar is complex conjugation, and the norm \(\parallel\psi\parallel^2 = \langle\psi,\psi\rangle\). As Fisher-Rao charges the variance of the rate, charge the variance of \(H\) in the state \(\psi\), the energy variance

\[\mathrm{Var}_\psi(H) = \parallel H\psi\parallel^2 - \langle\psi,H\psi\rangle^2 ,\]

where \(\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle\) is real because \(H\) is self-adjoint. A constant \(H\) only turns the global phase and costs nothing, which is why the state is the ray. Many \(H\) produce the same change, since only \(H\psi\) matters, but they all cost the same, so the cheapest price needs no minimising: with \(\dot\psi = -iH\psi\) it is the Fubini-Study metric on the velocity \(\dot\psi(x) = \partial\psi(x,t)/\partial t\),

\[g^{FS}_\psi(\dot\psi,\dot\psi) = \parallel\dot\psi\parallel^2 - \lvert\langle\psi,\dot\psi\rangle\rvert^2 ,\]

the length of a motion once the part that only rotates the global phase is removed. Fisher-Rao charges the variance of the growth rate; Fubini-Study charges the variance of the energy. Flow matching in this geometry has been built for quantum states, with phase-aligned paths between rays, and reports large gains over flow matching on the flat real and imaginary parts.

Geodesics. Multiply \(\psi_1\) by a constant phase so that \(\langle\psi_0,\psi_1\rangle\) is real and nonnegative; it then equals the fidelity \(F(\psi_0,\psi_1)\). The geodesic is the same slerp as for Fisher-Rao, now applied to the amplitudes,

\[\psi_t(x) = a_t\,\psi_0(x) + b_t\,\psi_1(x),\]

with \(a_t, b_t\) as for Fisher-Rao, where \(\Omega\) is now the angle between the amplitudes, \(\cos\Omega = F(\psi_0,\psi_1)\), and distance \(d_{FS}(\psi_0,\psi_1) = \Omega\). (By convention this is half the Fisher-Rao value, so compare \(d_{FS}\) with \(\tfrac12 d_{FR}\).)

Geodesics, side by side

Squaring the Fubini-Study geodesic gives its density:

\[\begin{aligned} \mu_t(x) &= a_t^2\,\mu_0(x) + b_t^2\,\mu_1(x) \\ &\quad + 2a_t b_t\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\overline{\psi_0(x)}\,\psi_1(x)\big), \end{aligned}\]

where \(a_t, b_t\) are the slerp coefficients above. The first two terms are the Fisher-Rao cross-fade. The third is an interference term, which can be negative: where it cancels the other two, the density vanishes. Where the relative phase of the endpoints varies with \(x\), it alternates in sign and makes fringes. A nonnegative geometry cannot produce it, because nonnegative combinations of nonnegative amplitudes are nonnegative.

Four geodesics from the same source, a Gaussian amplitude, to a target you choose. The separation $d$ starts at $2\sigma$, twice the width of each bump, so that source and target overlap; drag it to pull them apart. The targets:
  • A Gaussian: both amplitudes are real and nonnegative, so the Fubini-Study row is the Fisher-Rao row.
  • A wave packet (shown first): the Gaussian target multiplied by $e^{ikx}$. Its density is unchanged, so the first three rows do not change, but the Fubini-Study row gains fringes where the bumps overlap.
  • A signed state with one zero: the density rows see two bumps with a gap, which the exact Wasserstein path opens at once and the Fubini-Study path makes by cancellation.

The fringes are made by cancellation, not by moving mass: they come from the relative phase between the overlapping parts, which Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao cannot see and Fubini-Study charges for.

Fisher-Rao is the part the density sees

Fubini-Study looks like Fisher-Rao with a phase added. Writing the amplitude in polar form, \(\psi(x) = \sqrt{\mu(x)}\, e^{i\varphi(x)}\), with a phase \(\varphi(x)\) at every point, makes this exact: the metric splits into a part that changes the density and a part that changes only the phase,

\[g^{FS}_\psi(\dot\psi,\dot\psi) = \tfrac14\, g^{FR}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) + \mathrm{Var}_\mu(\dot\varphi) .\]

Here \(\dot\mu(x)\) and \(\dot\varphi(x)\) are the changes of density and of phase that \(\dot\psi\) produces, and \(\mathrm{Var}_\mu(\dot\varphi)\) is the variance of \(\dot\varphi(x)\) when \(x\) is drawn from \(\mu\).

A geodesic is a path of whole densities, but it also moves samples. In one dimension the continuity equation \(\frac{\partial\mu(x,t)}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(\mu(x,t)\,v(x,t)\big) = 0\) fixes the velocity,

\[v(x,t) = -\frac{1}{\mu(x,t)}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{\partial\mu(y,t)}{\partial t}\,dy ,\]

and a sample at position \(X_t\) that follows it, \(dX_t = v(X_t,t)\,dt\), keeps its quantile: the fraction of mass below it.

The figure shows this along the Fubini-Study geodesic, next to the amplitude itself. Where the interference term of the side-by-side section makes fringes or zeros, the samples bunch together and part around them. The samples respond only to the size of the amplitude, never to its phase. The path of densities is smooth, but the samples that carry it need not be: they move fastest where the density is smallest, and cross an exact zero instantly, where the phase at their position flips.

The density panel of the figure above shows the first part at work. Watch the colour rather than the samples: the source bump fades where it stands while the target grows where it stands, and where a zero forms it is made by cancellation, rather than by mass moving out of its way (choose the signed state). The density changes by birth and death, priced as Fisher-Rao prices it, and nothing in the price moves mass. The samples drawn on it do move, but only because in one dimension following the quantiles is the one way for particles to carry a density that changes in place.

Two consequences follow.

  • On nonnegative endpoints the two flows coincide. For \(\psi_0(x) = \sqrt{\mu_0(x)}\) and \(\psi_1(x) = \sqrt{\mu_1(x)}\) the great circle stays nonnegative, so the Fubini-Study geodesic is the Fisher-Rao geodesic, and the sphere used by Fisher Flow Matching is the nonnegative part of this one.
  • A phase mismatch only adds distance. \(F(\psi_0,\psi_1) \le \mathrm{BC}(\mu_0,\mu_1)\), with equality when the relative phase is constant where both amplitudes are nonzero, so \(d_{FS}(\psi_0,\psi_1) \ge \tfrac12\, d_{FR}(\mu_0,\mu_1)\).

Two points, the Bloch sphere

Where do the fringes come from, and when do they appear? A Fubini-Study geodesic only ever mixes its two endpoints, the source \(\psi_0\) and the target \(\psi_1\): every state along it is \(a_t\psi_0 + b_t\psi_1\). So to understand one path it is enough to understand every combination of two amplitudes. That set of states can be drawn as a sphere. To draw it we describe it with two orthogonal basis states, \(e_1\) and \(e_2\), which become the sphere’s poles. They are not the endpoints, which are generally not orthogonal.

Start with two points, two categories. Write \(e_1\) for the amplitude with all its mass on point 1 and \(e_2\) for all its mass on point 2. Fixing the global phase, every amplitude is \(\psi = \cos\tfrac\vartheta2\, e_1 + e^{i\gamma}\sin\tfrac\vartheta2\, e_2\), where \(\vartheta \in [0, \pi]\) sets the weights of the two components and \(\gamma\) is their relative phase, a single number. The density is \((\cos^2\tfrac\vartheta2, \sin^2\tfrac\vartheta2)\), and the split above gives

\[g^{FS}_\psi(\dot\psi,\dot\psi) = \tfrac14\left(\dot\vartheta^2 + \sin^2\vartheta\,\dot\gamma^2\right),\]

the round sphere of radius \(\tfrac12\), known as the Bloch sphere. Height on the sphere is the density and longitude is the relative phase \(\gamma\). Nonnegative amplitudes (\(\gamma = 0\)) form a half-meridian from pole to pole: this arc is Fisher-Rao, an edge of the eighth-sphere in the earlier figure. Signed real amplitudes (\(\gamma \in \{0, \pi\}\)) form the full meridian, and complex amplitudes fill the sphere.

Nothing in this needs \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) to be points. Any two orthonormal amplitudes, functions of \(x\) with \(\int\lvert e(x)\rvert^2\,dx = 1\) and \(\int\overline{e_1(x)}\,e_2(x)\,dx = 0\), span the same sphere, and its great circles are Fubini-Study geodesics. Every Fubini-Study geodesic lies on such a sphere. Take \(e_1 = \psi_0\), and for \(e_2\) the part of \(\psi_1\) orthogonal to \(\psi_0\), scaled to unit norm. The source then sits at a pole, and the target at polar angle \(\vartheta = 2\Omega\), where \(\cos\Omega = F(\psi_0,\psi_1)\), since \(\psi_1 = \cos\Omega\,e_1 + \sin\Omega\,e_2\): at the opposite pole only if the endpoints are orthogonal. The geodesic is the meridian between them.

Orthogonal is not the same as disjoint. Two points never overlap, so height on the sphere is the density and the phase is invisible. Functions can overlap point by point and still be orthogonal, and then the density gains a cross term,

\[\begin{aligned} \lvert\psi(x)\rvert^2 &= \cos^2\tfrac\vartheta2\,\lvert e_1(x)\rvert^2 + \sin^2\tfrac\vartheta2\,\lvert e_2(x)\rvert^2 \\ &\quad + \sin\vartheta\;\mathrm{Re}\big(e^{i\gamma}\,\overline{e_1(x)}\,e_2(x)\big). \end{aligned}\]

At each \(x\), \(\psi(x)\) is the sum of two arrows in the complex plane, and the cross term is the law of cosines: where the arrows line up the density grows, and where they oppose it shrinks. If \(e_2\) carries a wave \(e^{ikx}\), its arrow turns as \(x\) moves, and the cross term alternates in sign: the fringes of the side-by-side figure. Because \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) are orthogonal, the cross term integrates to zero: it changes where the density is without changing the total.

The Bloch sphere for a choice of basis; $e_1$ and $e_2$ sit at the poles. Drag $\psi_0$ and $\psi_1$, turn the sphere, and press play: the blue great circle is the Fubini-Study geodesic. Right: the density of $\psi_t$, as the sum of three terms, the part from $e_1$, the part from $e_2$ and the cross term (blue where it adds, red where it removes); the line is the sum of the ticked terms. All three give the density $\lvert\psi_t(x)\rvert^2$, of area one; the two parts have areas $a^2$ and $b^2$, and the cross term adds none, so on its own it can push the line below zero. Below, the value of $\psi(x)$ at the chosen point, a complex number, as the sum of two arrows: $A(x) = a\,e_1(x)$ and $B(x) = b\,e^{i\gamma}e_2(x)$. The squared length of the sum is the density there, and the angle between the arrows sets the sign of the cross term. The bases are the source of the side-by-side figure with each of its targets made orthogonal to it; for the wave packet, $e_1$ is the Gaussian bump at $x = -0.7$ and $e_2$ the bump at $x = 0.7$ carrying $e^{6ix}$, a change of order $10^{-4}$. With two separate bumps the cross term vanishes, so the phase is invisible and this is the two-point case drawn on a line; with the wave packet it makes fringes, which slide as the phase changes; with the signed state the relative phase decides which side of the zero gains density. Moving along a latitude changes $\gamma$, which turns the arrow $B(x)$ by the same angle at every $x$. The preset "the side-by-side path" is the Fubini-Study row of the side-by-side figure.

The two-point sphere also shows what happens at a density’s boundary. A distribution with all its mass on one point is a corner of the simplex, but on the Bloch sphere it is a pole, an ordinary point. A nonnegative path must end there; a signed or complex path can pass through.

The phase is invisible to samples

In practice a data point \(\mu\) is often known only through samples drawn from it. Suppose they are samples from \(\mu(x) = \lvert\psi(x)\rvert^2\). Their distribution depends on \(\mu\) alone, so every statistic computed from them is unchanged by any phase \(e^{i\chi(x)}\). This covers likelihoods, MMD, sliced Wasserstein distances and classifier-based scores. At its best, a flow that also carries a phase fits such data exactly as well as a flow on \(\mu\), and no better.

The phase becomes measurable as soon as the data include a second basis. A phase such as \(e^{ikx}\) leaves the position density alone but shifts the Fourier transform by \(k\). Physicists call the Fourier variable momentum. In general the Fourier density \(\lvert\hat\psi(p)\rvert^2\), where \(\hat\psi(p) = (2\pi)^{-1/2}\int\psi(x)\,e^{-ipx}\,dx\) is the Fourier transform at momentum \(p\), changes with \(\chi\). This is how quantum state tomography recovers a state from measurements in several bases. It is also why recovering a signal from the magnitude of a single Fourier transform is ill-posed, while overlapping short-time transforms usually pin it down.

Samples from a Gaussian amplitude with a phase $\chi$, against samples from the same Gaussian with no phase. The option $\chi = kx$ is the wave packet's phase. In position, the two-sample test separates them only at its false-positive rate, whatever the phase. In momentum (the Fourier variable) it separates them once the phase is strong enough: $kx$ shifts the momentum distribution, and a chirp widens it.

Geometry and representation

So far Fubini-Study has been a geometry: a metric, and the geodesics it puts in the loss. It also changes a second thing, the representation, meaning the function that the network outputs and the flow carries. The two are separate choices. Fisher Flow Matching already makes a representation choice. Fisher-Rao in \(\mu\) and Fisher-Rao in \(\sqrt\mu\) are the same geometry in different coordinates, and the square root is chosen because the geodesics, and the outputs, are simpler there.

The representation matters most at a node, a point where the density vanishes. Take \(\mu(x) \propto x^2 e^{-x^2}\). The density is smooth and touches zero at the origin. Its nonnegative root \(\sqrt{\mu(x)} \propto \lvert x\rvert\, e^{-x^2/2}\) has a kink there, while the signed root \(\psi(x) \propto x\, e^{-x^2/2}\) is smooth and has the same density.

A network with smooth activations, or any smooth parameterisation, fits the signed root far more easily, because a kink makes the error of a smooth fit fall only slowly as its capacity grows. The other common outputs do no better at a node: \(\mu\) itself must stay nonnegative and touch its bound there, and \(\log\mu\), the natural output of a softmax, goes to minus infinity.

Zeros also separate the geometries along a path. Take a source with no zeros and a target with some, follow the geodesic between them, and ask what changes when a network, which outputs smooth functions, has to produce it.

Geodesics from a Gaussian with no zeros to a target with one, two or three zeros; red marks are zeros of $\mu_t$. The slider $\ell$ blurs each geometry's output at scale $\ell$, a simple stand-in for the limited resolution, or smoothness, of a network's output: the transport map for Wasserstein, $\sqrt\mu$ for Fisher-Rao and $\psi$ for Fubini-Study. At the default $\ell = 0.05$, Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao produce dips but no zeros, even at $t = 1$, while Fubini-Study keeps every zero, made by cancellation: for an odd target one node arrives from far away, and the others form in pairs partway along. Set $\ell = 0$ for the exact geodesics. Fisher-Rao then reaches its zeros only at $t = 1$, and Wasserstein has them from the first instant. The same $\ell$ blurs the Wasserstein map more severely than it blurs $\sqrt\mu$ or $\psi$, because each bump of the target comes from a narrow range of source points.

The three behaviours follow from the formulas. Along a Fisher-Rao geodesic, \(\sqrt{\mu_t(x)} = a_t\sqrt{\mu_0(x)} + b_t\sqrt{\mu_1(x)}\) with \(a_t, b_t \ge 0\) the slerp coefficients of that geodesic, so the density vanishes only where both endpoints vanish, which a source with no zeros never does; the mixture behaves the same way. Along a Fubini-Study geodesic, \(\psi_t(x) = a_t\psi_0(x) + b_t\psi_1(x)\) vanishes wherever the two terms cancel.

Wasserstein moves mass, and a flow that moves samples acts as a map \(T\), sending a source point \(z\) to \(T(z)\). In one dimension the density it produces is \(\mu(T(z)) = \mu_0(z)\big/\tfrac{dT(z)}{dz}\), so from a source with no zeros the target can vanish only where \(dT(z)/dz = \infty\). The exact optimal map has exactly that at each zero of the target, which is why the exact geodesic has its zeros from the first instant. A flow with a smooth velocity field, which is what flow matching learns, cannot: it is a diffeomorphism at every time, so from a source with no zeros it only approaches the target, with dips that deepen as the network sharpens.

The outputs differ in the same way. A smooth \(\sqrt\mu\) or \(\mu\) can only touch zero, and a touching zero disappears under the smallest upward error. A real, signed \(\psi\) passes through zero by changing sign, and a small error moves that zero but cannot remove it. (This needs the amplitude to be real: on a line a complex amplitude vanishes only where its real and imaginary parts vanish together, which a small complex error destroys.) Physics has long used networks that output signed or complex amplitudes directly, and flows that transport samples meet the same obstacle at sign changes.

None of this contradicts the previous section. Samples cannot tell \(\psi\) from \(\lvert\psi\rvert\), so choosing a signed root changes nothing the data can check. It changes what the network has to output. Fubini-Study therefore offers two things, which are worth keeping apart. Its geometry, the phase direction and the interference term, helps only when the data carry a sign or a phase. Its representation, a real amplitude allowed to change sign, can help whenever the densities have zeros. Whether an easier function to output also makes the flow easier to train is an empirical question, and the recipe does not answer it.

Which geometry for which data

The four geometries differ in what their distances respond to.

  • Wasserstein, when distances in the underlying space should matter. Bumps far apart are far apart, and the interpolant moves mass rather than teleporting it. Point clouds and spatial densities are examples.
  • Mixture, as the baseline. It is the simplest and is exact on the simplex, but its price ignores how much mass is already present, and its distance depends on the units of \(x\). Dirichlet Flow Matching reports that linear flow matching on the simplex behaves pathologically and was built to avoid it, and statistical-manifold flow matching benchmarks against it.
  • Fisher-Rao, when the categories are exchangeable. Its distance ignores where mass sits, does not depend on units, and saturates at \(\pi\) once the supports separate. Categorical data are the natural case.
  • Fubini-Study, when the sign or phase is part of the object: quantum states measured in several bases, STFT frames, MRI \(k\)-space, eigenfunctions that change sign. On samples of a density its geometry adds nothing, as above. Its representation is a separate matter: when the densities have zeros, a real signed amplitude is the smoother function to output.

The recipe has three slots, the state, the rule and the price, and each can be filled in other ways. Two examples:

  • Both rules at once. Allow transport and birth and death together and add their prices: the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao geometry of unbalanced optimal transport. A length scale set by the relative price decides when moving mass is cheaper than destroying and recreating it.
  • Jumps on a graph. If the categories have structure (ordinal labels, neighbouring tokens), let mass hop between neighbours and price the hops like kinetic energy: Maas’s discrete Wasserstein distance, under which nearby categories are close, unlike under Fisher-Rao. The rule is a continuous-time Markov chain, the kind of process discrete flow matching samples, though there its state is a token rather than a distribution.

The mixture, Fisher-Rao and Fubini-Study geometries have closed-form geodesics, exponential and logarithm maps: the first is flat, Fisher-Rao is a sphere, and Fubini-Study is a sphere with global phases identified. Wasserstein has them in one dimension and between Gaussians. In these cases the Riemannian objective applies as it stands. The recipe identifies the space the data live in. It does not say which path through that space is easiest for a network to learn, which remains an empirical question for each kind of data.

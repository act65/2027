One recipe for the geometries of flow matching: from densities to amplitudes

Flow matching assumes your data lives in Euclidean space, but when each data point is a distribution, the straight line and the L2 norm need replacing, and Wasserstein, the linear mixture and Fisher-Rao are the usual choices. We show that all three fall out of one recipe, a rule for how probability mass may change plus a price for each change, and watch the same two bumps slide or cross-fade under each. Run on complex amplitudes, the same recipe gives a fourth geometry, Fubini-Study; samples cannot see its phase, but a signed amplitude lets a network represent zeros of a density that survive small errors.

Flow matching is Euclidean flow matching

Flow matching trains a vector field \(v_\theta\) to transform a source distribution to a data distribution. In its standard form it draws a source point \(x_0\), a data point \(x_1\) and a time \(t \sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1]\), joins the two points by a straight line, and regresses \(v_\theta\) onto the line’s velocity:

\[\mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\, \parallel v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \parallel^2,\] \[x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1 .\]

Two choices in this loss are Euclidean: the interpolant is a chord, the straight line through the ambient space, and the error is measured in the Euclidean norm. Riemannian flow matching keeps the form of the loss and replaces both on a manifold with metric \(g\). The chord becomes the geodesic, and the Euclidean norm becomes the metric at the current point:

\[\mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\, \parallel v_\theta(x_t, t) - \dot x_t \parallel^2_{g(x_t)},\] \[x_t = \exp_{x_0}\!\big(t \log_{x_0} x_1\big), \qquad \dot x_t = \frac{\log_{x_t} x_1}{1-t}.\]

Here \(\log_x y\) is the initial velocity of the geodesic from \(x\) to \(y\), scaled so that its length is their distance, and \(\exp_x\) walks along the geodesic with a given initial velocity. These are the exponential and logarithm maps of the manifold, not the elementwise functions: in Euclidean space \(\exp_x(\delta) = x + \delta\) and \(\log_x y = y - x\), which gives back the first loss. A geometry enters the loss through two objects: the interpolant \(x_t\) and the norm \(\parallel \cdot\parallel_{g}\). They are not equally important. Because \(g\) depends only on \(x_t\), the best \(v_\theta\) is \(\mathbb E[\dot x_t \mid x_t]\) whatever the norm, so the norm only reweights the regression. The interpolant decides what is learned.

Flow matching between distributions over distributions

In this post every data point is itself a distribution \(\mu\): a whole density takes the place that the vector \(x_t\) had above. From here on, \(x\) is no longer a data point but a position, the variable a density is a function of, as in \(\mu(x)\). For categorical data with \(K\) classes, \(\mu\) is a vector of \(K\) probabilities, the source distribution can be uniform over such vectors, and \(v_\theta\) outputs a velocity \(\dot\mu\) whose entries sum to zero. In Fisher and Dirichlet flow matching the data are one-hot vectors, the corners of the simplex, and only the path between them passes through general distributions; statistical-manifold and Wasserstein flow matching also take data points that are themselves general distributions. For a parametric family, such as the Gaussians, \(\mu\) is named by its parameters. For the pictures below, \(\mu\) is a density on the line, stored on a grid, and the running example is the Gaussians \(\mathcal N(m, w^2)\) on the line, each a point of the plane in Figure 1. In every case the flow runs on a space of distributions, and each point of it is a whole density.

So there are two levels. One level up, the flow’s source and data distributions, call them \(P_0\) and \(P_1\), are distributions over densities: a training pair is a draw \(\mu_0 \sim P_0\) and a draw \(\mu_1 \sim P_1\), the interpolant is the geodesic from one to the other, and the loss averages over many such pairs. Within a pair we call \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu_1\) the start and end densities, or the endpoints; “source” and “data” always mean \(P_0\) and \(P_1\). One level down, each density \(\mu(x)\) is itself a distribution over positions \(x\); draws \(X \sim \mu\) from it are what this post calls samples. Figures 2, 3, 6, 9 and 10 each show a single training pair, that is, one term of the loss; Figures 4 and 7 show the field learned from many.

Samples $X \sim \mu$ live one level down, inside each point.

In symbols, a data point is a density \(\mu(x)\) over \(x\), a path of data points is a density \(\mu(x,t)\) that changes with \(t\) (also written \(\mu_t(x)\)), and its velocity at time \(t\) is the function

\[\dot\mu(x) = \frac{\partial \mu(x,t)}{\partial t}, \qquad \int \dot\mu(x)\,dx = 0 ,\]

where the integral is zero because the total mass stays one. This \(\dot\mu\) plays the role of \(\dot x_t\) in the loss above, and a metric \(g_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu)\) is a rule that assigns this velocity a squared length, which may depend on where the path is, \(\mu\).

Existing work fills in the metric \(g\) in two ways. Moving mass gives the Wasserstein geometry, used by Wasserstein Flow Matching. Creating and destroying mass in place gives the geometries of simplex flow matching: the flat geometry of linear mixtures, the usual baseline, and the Fisher-Rao geometry of Fisher Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching. All of them come from one recipe, which the next section sets out.

A geometry here is a rule plus a price, and one recipe turns them into all four geometries; moving from densities to amplitudes adds exactly one new direction, a phase, which samples cannot see, so what it offers sample data is a representation rather than a new geometry.

One recipe

Flow matching always needs two things: an interpolant between a source point and a data point, such as the straight line above, a Gaussian conditional path or a stochastic interpolant, and a norm to measure the error of a velocity. Here the two points are densities, \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu_1\), so the interpolant is a path of densities and the velocity is \(\dot\mu\). Many paths join two densities, so we want the shortest one, and “shortest” needs a length for every velocity: a metric \(g_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu)\). Each geometry below gets its metric and geodesic in the same four steps. They act on a state, the thing that changes along the path: for now the density \(\mu\) itself; later, an amplitude. The state, the rule and the price are choices; the cheapest price and the geodesic follow from them.

A rule. Say how probability mass may change. The rule takes a control \(u\), whatever we are allowed to adjust, and turns it into a change of density. Applied for a short time \(\tau\), starting from \(\mu\), it gives a new density

\[\tilde\mu(x) = \mu(x) + \tau\,\dot\mu(x), \qquad \dot\mu = R_\mu(u),\]

where \(R_\mu\) is the rule written as a map from controls to changes. We ask three things of it: it is linear in \(u\) (twice the control gives twice the change), it conserves total mass, \(\int\dot\mu(x)\,dx = 0\), and it keeps \(\tilde\mu\) nonnegative.

A price. Charge for the control, not for the change: a cost \(c_\mu(u)\) for applying \(u\) at \(\mu\). We ask that it is quadratic in \(u\), so that the length it defines comes from an inner product and the loss is a weighted least squares; that it charges \(u\) and \(-u\) alike; and that it is positive unless the control leaves the state unchanged.

The cheapest price. Now ask what it costs to get from \(\mu\) to \(\tilde\mu\). Several controls may lead there, and the squared length of the change is the price of the cheapest one:

\[g_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = \min\big\{\, c_\mu(u) \;:\; R_\mu(u) = \dot\mu \,\big\} .\]

The minimum has work to do only when different controls produce the same change at different prices: transport in two or more dimensions, where many velocity fields move mass the same way. Elsewhere either the change fixes the control (birth and death) or all controls with the same effect cost the same (amplitudes), and the price alone sets the metric.

Geodesics. With a length for every velocity, the geodesic from \(\mu_0\) to \(\mu_1\) is the path whose total price \(\int_0^1 g_{\mu_t}(\dot\mu_t,\dot\mu_t)\,dt\) is smallest; that smallest total is the squared distance between them. For transport this is optimal transport: the cheapest way to move one density onto another.

Fix a rule for how probability mass may change, and a price for changing it. The metric is the cheapest price of a given change. Flow matching then uses the metric’s geodesics as interpolants and its norm in the loss.

None of these geometries is new, and neither is the mathematics behind the recipe. What this post adds is the single frame: the geometries of flow matching on distributions side by side, derived the same way on one example, the recipe run once more on amplitudes, and the line it draws between a geometry and the function a network outputs. The next sections run the recipe on densities, where it reproduces the geometries that flow matching already uses, on one running example; after that we run it on amplitudes, a second way to represent a density, where it gives Fubini-Study.

Transport gives Wasserstein

Rule. Mass moves with a velocity field \(U(x,t)\) and is conserved locally (for \(x \in \mathbb R^n\); \(\nabla\cdot\) is the divergence, the net outflow, and \(\nabla\) the gradient):

\[\frac{\partial \mu(x,t)}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\big(\mu(x,t)\,U(x,t)\big) = 0 .\]

This is the continuity (transport) equation, the same equation that links the probability path of any flow matching model to its vector field. Here it acts one level down, inside a single data point. The density \(\mu(x,t)\) is a single data point at time \(t\), a function of position \(x\) on the line, and \(U(x,t)\) says where its mass moves; the equation describes one step along the path between two data points.

Cheapest price. Here, unlike for the other rules, several controls produce the same change, so the cheapest one has to be found. Adding to \(U(x)\) any field \(\eta(x)\) with \(\nabla\cdot\big(\mu(x)\,\eta(x)\big) = 0\), a flow that circulates without piling up mass anywhere, moves the mass differently but leaves the change of density \(\dot\mu(x)\) the same. Charge kinetic energy, \(\int \parallel U(x)\parallel^2\,\mu(x)\,dx\), the squared speed of the mass averaged over where the mass is. A circulating part is orthogonal to a gradient field under this price, so it only adds energy and moves nothing; the cheapest field has none, and is a gradient, \(U(x) = \nabla\xi(x)\). The potential \(\xi(x)\) is an ordinary function of \(x\), fixed by \(\mu\) and \(\dot\mu\) through the second equation below. The price is Otto’s metric

\[g^{W}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = \int \parallel\nabla\xi(x)\parallel^2\,\mu(x)\,dx,\] \[-\nabla\cdot\big(\mu(x)\,\nabla\xi(x)\big) = \dot\mu(x).\]

Geodesics. With this metric, the length of the shortest path is the 2-Wasserstein distance \(W_2(\mu_0,\mu_1)\). Along it every particle moves in a straight line at constant speed (displacement interpolation). This is the interpolant in the loss. It is closed form in one dimension, where it interpolates the quantile functions. Wasserstein Flow Matching uses the closed form between Gaussians and entropic estimates between point clouds.

Between two Gaussians. The Wasserstein path between two Gaussians stays Gaussian. It is McCann’s displacement interpolation: on the line the optimal map between two Gaussians is affine, so every particle moves in a straight line, and the mean and the width both change linearly in time. In the plane of Figure 2 the path is the straight segment between the endpoints, and over \(x\) the bump slides and stretches without changing shape.

Birth and death give the mixture

Rule. Mass may not move. It may only appear or disappear in place, at a rate \(r(x,t)\):

\[\frac{\partial \mu(x,t)}{\partial t} = r(x,t)\,\mu(x,t),\] \[\int r(x,t)\,\mu(x,t)\,dx = 0 ,\]

where the zero-mean condition keeps the total mass at one. Unlike transport, there is no cheapest control to find: the change fixes the rate, \(r(x) = \dot\mu(x)/\mu(x)\), so the price alone sets the metric.

Flat price. Charge the change itself, \(\int \big(r(x)\,\mu(x)\big)^2\,dx\). The metric is

\[g^{L^2}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = \int \dot\mu(x)^2\, dx ,\]

the same at every \(\mu\), so the geometry is flat.

Geodesics. Straight lines, \(\mu_t(x) = (1-t)\,\mu_0(x) + t\,\mu_1(x)\), the mixture path. A mixture of densities is a density, so the path never leaves the simplex. In the loss, this is Euclidean flow matching on the probability vector: the interpolant is linear, the regression target is \(\mu_1 - \mu_0\) at every \(t\), and the norm is Euclidean. It is the linear baseline that Dirichlet Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching compare against.

Between two Gaussians. The mixture path leaves the Gaussians at once: \((1-t)\,\mu_0(x) + t\,\mu_1(x)\) is a mixture of the two endpoints, which stay where they are while their weights trade off. Nothing moves; one bump fades as the other grows, and in between the density has two bumps.

Fisher’s price gives Fisher-Rao

Same rule, other price. Keep birth and death, and charge the rate instead of the change: the variance of the rate, \(\int r(x)^2\,\mu(x)\,dx\). This gives the Fisher-Rao metric,

\[g^{FR}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = \int \frac{\dot\mu(x)^2}{\mu(x)}\, dx .\]

Compared with the flat price it divides by \(\mu\), so changing mass that is nearly zero is expensive. It is also the curvature of the KL divergence: for a small step, \(\mathrm{KL}\big(\mu \,\|\, \mu + \tau\dot\mu\big) = \tfrac12\tau^2\, g^{FR}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) + O(\tau^3)\). Up to scale, it is the only metric that is unchanged when categories are merged or split, which is Chentsov’s theorem. For densities on a closed space of two or more dimensions it is also the only one unchanged by any smooth relabelling of \(x\); our pictures, on a line, fall outside that result. This invariance is what the flat price lacked.

The square root. In the coordinates \(s(x) = \sqrt{\mu(x)}\) the velocity is \(\dot s(x) = \dot\mu(x)/\big(2\sqrt{\mu(x)}\big)\), so

\[g^{FR}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) = 4\int \dot s(x)^2\,dx .\]

Since \(\int s(x)^2\,dx = \int\mu(x)\,dx = 1\), the square roots lie on the unit sphere, and Fisher-Rao is four times its round metric: densities fill the sphere’s nonnegative part. This is why Fisher Flow Matching and statistical-manifold flow matching replace \(\mu\) by \(\sqrt\mu\) before anything else. The mixture is flat in \(\mu\); Fisher-Rao is round in \(\sqrt\mu\).

Geodesics. Great circles on that sphere: the interpolant in the loss is the spherical interpolation (slerp) of \(\sqrt{\mu_0}\) and \(\sqrt{\mu_1}\) through the angle \(\Omega\) between them,

\[\sqrt{\mu_t(x)} = a_t\sqrt{\mu_0(x)} + b_t\sqrt{\mu_1(x)},\] \[a_t = \frac{\sin((1-t)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega},\qquad b_t = \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}.\]

Its cosine is the overlap of the two square roots, \(\cos\Omega = \mathrm{BC}(\mu_0,\mu_1) = \int\sqrt{\mu_0(x)\,\mu_1(x)}\,dx\), known as the Bhattacharyya coefficient: it is \(1\) when the densities are equal and \(0\) when they do not overlap. The distance is \(d_{FR}(\mu_0,\mu_1) = 2\Omega\), twice the angle because of the factor 4 in the metric. Because small masses are expensive, the path slows down near the edges of the simplex.

Between two Gaussians. The Fisher-Rao path is also a mixture, but of three Gaussians. Squaring the slerp gives \(a_t^2\,\mu_0(x) + b_t^2\,\mu_1(x) + 2a_tb_t\sqrt{\mu_0(x)\,\mu_1(x)}\), and between two Gaussians \(\sqrt{\mu_0(x)\,\mu_1(x)}\) is itself proportional to a Gaussian \(\nu(x)\) lying between them, with weight at most \(\mathrm{BC}(\mu_0,\mu_1)\). While the endpoints overlap, the path grows a third bump in the middle; when they are far apart the overlap is small and the path is close to the mixture’s cross-fade.

Each edge of the triangle is the two-category simplex.

Same reach, different price. None of these rules restricts where a positive density can go. Birth and death produce any change of total mass zero (\(r(x) = \dot\mu(x)/\mu(x)\)), and so does transport (solve \(-\nabla\cdot\big(\mu(x)\nabla\xi(x)\big) = \dot\mu(x)\) for \(\xi\)). The rules and prices differ only in how a change is paid for, and therefore in which path is cheapest. The mixture and Fisher-Rao make the point most sharply: one rule, two prices, two geometries.

What flow matching learns. Every figure so far shows one training pair. Flow matching does not learn any single path: it learns a field \(v_\theta(\mu, t)\) over all densities, and the loss is minimised when, at each density and time, \(v_\theta\) is the average velocity of the training pairs whose geodesics pass through that density. Which pairs are drawn is a choice of the method; here they are matched by optimal transport, each geometry using its own distance as the cost. To draw this field we need a space that every geodesic stays in. Densities built from a few bumps are one. Fix \(k\) bump densities \(b_1(x), \dots, b_k(x)\) that do not overlap, and take every mixture of them, \(\mu(x) = p_1 b_1(x) + \dots + p_k b_k(x)\), with weights \(p_i \ge 0\) that sum to one. Each such density is a point \((p_1, \dots, p_k)\) of the probability simplex, a triangle for \(k = 3\) as in Figure 3, and both the mixture and the Fisher-Rao geodesics between two of them stay in it: the mixture’s because a mixture of mixtures is a mixture, and Fisher-Rao’s because, with no overlap, \(\sqrt{\mu(x)} = \sqrt{p_1}\, \sqrt{b_1(x)} + \dots + \sqrt{p_k}\, \sqrt{b_k(x)}\) is built from the same bumps. Wasserstein’s do not, because they move mass between the bumps, through densities that are not built from them.

Figure 4. The field that flow matching learns on densities built from three bumps that do not overlap, each density a point of the triangle; click a corner to see its bump. The source distribution $P_0$ holds densities with most of their weight on the first bump; the data distribution $P_1$ has two clusters. Pairs are matched by minibatch optimal transport, with the chosen geometry's own squared distance as the cost, and joined by its geodesics; the arrows are the average velocity of the pairs passing through each density at time $t$, the minimiser of the loss, which $v_\theta$ approximates. The mixture field moves weight along straight lines; the Fisher-Rao field bends, because it charges more for changing small weights. On the right, the followed point as a density over $x$.

An amplitude gives Fubini-Study

So far the recipe has acted on the density itself. A density is nonnegative, so all three geometries live in the nonnegative orthant of the square-root sphere. That is one way to hold a density, but not the only one. An amplitude \(\psi(x)\), a signed or complex function, also gives a density, through the Born rule \(\mu(x) = \lvert\psi(x)\rvert^2\), and many amplitudes give the same one. Some data are amplitudes in their own right: a short-time Fourier transform frame, an MRI measurement or an eigenfunction that changes sign, where the sign or phase carries information. Normalising such an amplitude keeps its shape; its overall scale, a loudness or an intensity, is a separate number to model alongside it. So change the state from the density to the amplitude, and run the recipe again. The geometry it gives, Fubini-Study, is already in use: flow matching for quantum states builds phase-aligned paths between rays in it, and reports large gains over flow matching on the flat real and imaginary parts.

Five quantum words, for ML readers
  • Amplitude $\psi(x)$: a complex function with $\int |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1$. Its density is $\mu(x) = |\psi(x)|^2$, the Born rule.
  • Global phase: multiplying $\psi(x)$ by a constant $e^{i\alpha}$. It changes neither the density nor anything used below.
  • Ray: an amplitude up to its global phase, that is, the set of all $e^{i\alpha}\psi(x)$. Quotienting by this circle of phases is "modulo $U(1)$".
  • Hamiltonian $H$: a self-adjoint operator that generates the motion, $\frac{\partial \psi(x,t)}{\partial t} = -iH\psi(x,t)$. Here it is only a control, like $U$ and $r$ above; no physics is assumed.
  • Fidelity $F(\psi_0,\psi_1) = |\langle\psi_0, \psi_1\rangle |$: the overlap of two rays, the inner product once a global phase has been chosen to make it real and nonnegative; the analogue of BC.

State. A complex amplitude \(\psi(x)\) with density \(\mu(x) = \lvert\psi(x)\rvert^2\). Every density is reached (\(\psi(x) = \sqrt{\mu(x)}\)), but many amplitudes give the same density: \(e^{i\chi(x)}\psi(x)\) does, for any real function \(\chi(x)\), a change of the phase at each point. A constant phase, \(e^{i\alpha}\psi(x)\), changes nothing used below, so the state is the amplitude up to this global phase, called a ray and written \([\psi]\).

Where the phase enters. Write the amplitude as \(\psi(x) = \sqrt{\mu(x)}\, e^{i\varphi(x)}\), with a phase \(\varphi(x)\) at every point. On its own a phase never changes the density: \(\lvert\psi(x)\rvert^2 = \mu(x)\) whatever \(\varphi(x)\) is. It matters when amplitudes are added, as they are along a geodesic or in a combination of basis states. Each part of such a sum can carry a phase in two ways: within itself, varying with \(x\), like the winding \(e^{ikx}\) of a wave packet or the sign flip of a signed state; or as a whole, a constant phase relative to the other parts. Where two parts overlap, the density gains a cross term that depends on their relative phase at each \(x\); where they do not overlap, neither kind of phase shows in the density. The figures below use both kinds, and say which.

Rule. Conservation of mass becomes conservation of the norm, \(\int\lvert\psi(x,t)\rvert^2\,dx = 1\). The linear evolutions that preserve it are \(\frac{\partial \psi(x,t)}{\partial t} = -iH\psi(x,t)\) with \(H\) self-adjoint (the analogue of a symmetric matrix): the Schrödinger equation, used here only as the general norm-preserving rule, with \(H\) as the control.

Price. Use the inner product \(\langle\psi,\phi\rangle = \int \overline{\psi(x)}\,\phi(x)\,dx\), where the bar is complex conjugation, and the norm \(\parallel\psi\parallel^2 = \langle\psi,\psi\rangle\). As Fisher-Rao charges the variance of the rate, charge the variance of \(H\) in the state \(\psi(x)\), the energy variance

\[\mathrm{Var}_\psi(H) = \parallel H\psi\parallel^2 - \langle\psi,H\psi\rangle^2 ,\]

where \(\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle\) is real because \(H\) is self-adjoint. A constant \(H\) only turns the global phase and costs nothing, which is why the state is the ray. Many \(H\) produce the same change, since only \(H\psi(x)\) matters, but they all cost the same, so the cheapest price needs no minimising: with \(\dot\psi(x) = -iH\psi(x)\) it is the Fubini-Study metric on the velocity \(\dot\psi(x) = \partial\psi(x,t)/\partial t\),

\[g^{FS}_\psi(\dot\psi,\dot\psi) = \parallel\dot\psi\parallel^2 - \lvert\langle\psi,\dot\psi\rangle\rvert^2 ,\]

the length of a motion once the part that only rotates the global phase is removed. Fisher-Rao charges the variance of the growth rate; Fubini-Study charges the variance of the energy.

Geodesics. The overlap of two rays is the fidelity,

\[F(\psi_0,\psi_1) = \lvert\langle\psi_0,\psi_1\rangle\rvert = \Big\lvert \int \overline{\psi_0(x)}\,\psi_1(x)\,dx \Big\rvert = \max_\alpha\, \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi_0, e^{i\alpha}\psi_1\rangle ,\]

the inner product after the best global phase alignment. Multiplying \(\psi_1(x)\) by \(e^{i\alpha}\) turns the complex number \(\langle\psi_0,\psi_1\rangle\) about the origin; turned onto the positive real axis, it equals \(F(\psi_0,\psi_1)\). Only one constant phase is aligned, not the phase at each \(x\): aligning every point separately would give \(\int\lvert\psi_0(x)\rvert\,\lvert\psi_1(x)\rvert\,dx = \mathrm{BC}(\mu_0,\mu_1)\), so \(F(\psi_0,\psi_1) \le \mathrm{BC}(\mu_0,\mu_1)\). Take \(\psi_1(x)\) with this aligned phase. The geodesic is the same slerp as for Fisher-Rao, now applied to the amplitudes,

\[\psi_t(x) = a_t\,\psi_0(x) + b_t\,\psi_1(x),\]

with \(a_t, b_t\) as for Fisher-Rao, where \(\Omega\) is now the angle between the amplitudes, \(\cos\Omega = F(\psi_0,\psi_1)\), and distance \(d_{FS}(\psi_0,\psi_1) = \Omega\). (By convention this is half the Fisher-Rao value, so compare \(d_{FS}\) with \(\tfrac12 d_{FR}\).)

Between two Gaussians. If both endpoints are Gaussian amplitudes with no phase, \(\psi_0(x) = \sqrt{\mu_0(x)}\) and \(\psi_1(x) = \sqrt{\mu_1(x)}\), the path is again a mixture of three Gaussians. A phase changes that. Give the end a phase within it, the winding \(e^{ikx}\) of a wave packet: its density is unchanged, but the cross term between the two bumps now oscillates in \(x\), so where they overlap the path carries fringes that neither endpoint has (the interference term below, and Figure 6).

Amplitudes can be built the same way, from \(k\) fixed basis states \(e_1(x), \dots, e_k(x)\). Take them orthonormal, \(\int \overline{e_i(x)}\, e_j(x)\, dx = 1\) when \(i = j\) and \(0\) otherwise (any \(k\) independent amplitudes can be made so by Gram-Schmidt), and take every combination \(\psi(x) = c_1 e_1(x) + \dots + c_k e_k(x)\) with complex coefficients \(c_i\). Orthonormality makes the norm \(\lvert c_1\rvert^2 + \dots + \lvert c_k\rvert^2\), which must be one, and the global phase is dropped as before. The space of all of them needs more than a triangle; it can be drawn in two parts: the weights \(\lvert c_i\rvert^2\), a point of the same triangle, and the \(k-1\) relative phases, a point of a torus. When the basis states do not overlap, the weights are the density and the phases are invisible to samples; when they overlap, the phases also shape the density, through interference. At an edge of the triangle one coefficient is zero and its phase is undefined, so the torus collapses there. A nonnegative path that reaches an edge must stop; a signed path can cross it, the coefficient passing through zero as its phase jumps by \(\pi\). For \(k = 2\) the triangle is a segment and the torus a circle, and the two together make a sphere, the Bloch sphere of quantum mechanics. For \(k = 3\) they are a triangle and a square (Figure 5).

Phases in a basis. A combination of basis states is a sum of parts, so the two kinds of phase can be set separately. Take two parts, \(\psi(x) = a\,\psi_0(x) + b\,\psi_1(x)\): a phase between the parts sits in the coefficients \(a\) and \(b\), and a phase within them sits in the functions \(\psi_0(x)\) and \(\psi_1(x)\). Table 1 sets out the four cases. With real parts, such as square roots of densities, complex coefficients give an amplitude over densities; with complex parts they give an amplitude over amplitudes. With \(k\) basis states the coefficients are the \(c_i\) and the parts are the states \(e_i(x)\).

Interference

A Fubini-Study geodesic only ever mixes its two endpoints: every state along it lies in their span. Squaring the Fubini-Study geodesic \(\psi_t(x) = a_t\,\psi_0(x) + b_t\,\psi_1(x)\) gives its density:

\[\begin{aligned} \mu_t(x) &= a_t^2\,\mu_0(x) + b_t^2\,\mu_1(x) \\ &\quad + 2a_t b_t\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\overline{\psi_0(x)}\,\psi_1(x)\big), \end{aligned}\]

where \(a_t, b_t\) are the slerp coefficients above. The first two terms are the Fisher-Rao cross-fade. The third is an interference term. At each \(x\), \(\psi_t(x)\) is the sum of two complex numbers, \(a_t\psi_0(x)\) and \(b_t\psi_1(x)\), and the interference term is the law of cosines: where the two point the same way the density grows, where they point in opposite directions it shrinks, and where they cancel it vanishes. Where the relative phase of the endpoints varies with \(x\), as it does when one of them carries a phase within it, the term alternates in sign and makes fringes (Figure 6). It needs the endpoints to overlap: where either one is zero the term is zero, so amplitudes that are orthogonal because they do not overlap never interfere, whatever their phases, while amplitudes that overlap and are still orthogonal can. A nonnegative geometry cannot cancel, because nonnegative combinations of nonnegative amplitudes are nonnegative.

The fringes are made by cancellation, not by moving mass: they come from the relative phase between the overlapping parts, which Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao cannot see and Fubini-Study charges for.

Fisher-Rao inside Fubini-Study

Rewrite the amplitude in polar form, \(\psi(x) = \sqrt{\mu(x)}\, e^{i\varphi(x)}\), and the Fubini-Study metric splits into a part that changes the density and a part that changes only the phase,

\[g^{FS}_\psi(\dot\psi,\dot\psi) = \tfrac14\, g^{FR}_\mu(\dot\mu,\dot\mu) + \mathrm{Var}_\mu(\dot\varphi) .\]

Here \(\dot\mu(x)\) and \(\dot\varphi(x)\) are the changes of density and of phase that \(\dot\psi(x)\) produces, and \(\mathrm{Var}_\mu(\dot\varphi)\) is the variance of \(\dot\varphi(x)\) when \(x\) is drawn from \(\mu\). The first term is the Fisher-Rao metric, a quarter of it. The second is the price of the phase. In the top-left corner of Table 1, with every part and coefficient nonnegative, there is no phase to change, the second term is zero, and Fubini-Study is Fisher-Rao: Fisher-Rao sits inside Fubini-Study as its no-phase corner.

The density panel of Figure 6 shows the first part at work: the start bump fades where it stands while the end grows where it stands, and where a zero forms it is made by cancellation, rather than by mass moving out of its way (choose the signed state). The density changes by birth and death, priced as Fisher-Rao prices it, and nothing in the price moves mass.

Three consequences follow.

  • In the no-phase corner the two flows coincide. For \(\psi_0(x) = \sqrt{\mu_0(x)}\) and \(\psi_1(x) = \sqrt{\mu_1(x)}\) the great circle stays nonnegative, so the Fubini-Study geodesic is the Fisher-Rao geodesic, and the sphere used by Fisher Flow Matching is the nonnegative part of this one. Figure 7, with basis b and no phases, shows it for a whole learned flow.
  • Real coefficients on other parts give Fisher-Rao on the weights. With orthonormal parts that carry phases of their own and nonnegative coefficients, the geodesic again never leaves the nonnegative coefficients, so it is Fisher-Rao on the weights \(\lvert c_i\rvert^2\); but where the parts overlap the weights are not the density (Figure 7, basis e and no phases).
  • Any phase only adds distance. Since \(F(\psi_0,\psi_1) \le \mathrm{BC}(\mu_0,\mu_1)\), with equality when the relative phase is constant where both amplitudes are nonzero, \(d_{FS}(\psi_0,\psi_1) \ge \tfrac12\, d_{FR}(\mu_0,\mu_1)\).

The phase is invisible to samples

In practice a data point \(\mu\) is often known only through samples drawn from it. Suppose they are samples from \(\mu(x) = \lvert\psi(x)\rvert^2\). Their distribution depends on \(\mu\) alone, so every statistic computed from them is unchanged by any phase \(e^{i\chi(x)}\). This covers likelihoods, MMD, sliced Wasserstein distances and classifier-based scores. At its best, a flow that also carries a phase fits such data exactly as well as a flow on \(\mu\), and no better.

The phase becomes measurable as soon as the data include a second basis. A phase such as \(e^{ikx}\) leaves the position density alone but shifts the Fourier transform by \(k\). Physicists call the Fourier variable momentum. In general the Fourier density \(\lvert\hat\psi(p)\rvert^2\), where \(\hat\psi(p) = (2\pi)^{-1/2}\int\psi(x)\,e^{-ipx}\,dx\) is the Fourier transform at momentum \(p\), changes with \(\chi\). This is how quantum state tomography recovers a state from measurements in several bases. It is also why recovering a signal from the magnitude of a single Fourier transform is ill-posed, while overlapping short-time transforms usually pin it down.

Figure 8. Samples from a Gaussian amplitude with a phase $\chi$ within it, against samples from the same Gaussian with no phase. With a single bump there is nothing for the phase to interfere with, so samples in position cannot see it. The option $\chi = kx$ is the wave packet's phase. In position, the two-sample test separates them only at its false-positive rate, whatever the phase. In momentum (the Fourier variable) it separates them once the phase is strong enough: $kx$ shifts the momentum distribution, and a chirp widens it.

Geometry and representation

So far Fubini-Study has been a geometry: a metric, and the geodesics it puts in the loss. It also changes a second thing, the representation, meaning the function that the network outputs and the flow carries. The two are separate choices. Fisher Flow Matching already makes a representation choice. Fisher-Rao in \(\mu\) and Fisher-Rao in \(\sqrt\mu\) are the same geometry in different coordinates, and the square root is chosen because the geodesics, and the outputs, are simpler there.

The representation matters most at a node, a point where the density vanishes. Take \(\mu(x) \propto x^2 e^{-x^2}\). The density is smooth and touches zero at the origin. Its nonnegative root \(\sqrt{\mu(x)} \propto \lvert x\rvert\, e^{-x^2/2}\) has a kink there, while the signed root \(\psi(x) \propto x\, e^{-x^2/2}\) is smooth and has the same density.

A network with smooth activations, or any smooth parameterisation, fits the signed root far more easily, because a kink makes the error of a smooth fit fall only slowly as its capacity grows. The other common outputs do no better at a node: \(\mu\) itself must stay nonnegative and touch its bound there, and \(\log\mu\), the natural output of a softmax, goes to minus infinity.

Zeros also separate the geometries along a path. Take a start density with no zeros and an end density with some, follow the geodesic between them, and ask what changes when a network, which outputs smooth functions, has to produce it.

Figure 9. Geodesics from a Gaussian with no zeros to an end with one, two or three zeros, a signed state whose sign flips within it; red marks are zeros of $\mu(x,t)$. The slider $\ell$ blurs each geometry's output at scale $\ell$, a simple stand-in for the limited resolution, or smoothness, of a network's output: the transport map for Wasserstein, $\sqrt{\mu(x)}$ for Fisher-Rao and $\psi(x)$ for Fubini-Study. At the default $\ell = 0.05$, Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao produce dips but no zeros, even at $t = 1$, while Fubini-Study keeps every zero, made by cancellation: for an odd end one node arrives from far away, and the others form in pairs partway along. Set $\ell = 0$ for the exact geodesics. Fisher-Rao then reaches its zeros only at $t = 1$, and Wasserstein has them from the first instant. The same $\ell$ blurs the Wasserstein map more severely than it blurs $\sqrt{\mu(x)}$ or $\psi(x)$, because each bump of the end comes from a narrow range of starting positions.

The three behaviours follow from the formulas. Along a Fisher-Rao geodesic, \(\sqrt{\mu_t(x)} = a_t\sqrt{\mu_0(x)} + b_t\sqrt{\mu_1(x)}\) with \(a_t, b_t \ge 0\) the slerp coefficients of that geodesic, so the density vanishes only where both endpoints vanish, which a start with no zeros never does; the mixture behaves the same way. Along a Fubini-Study geodesic, \(\psi_t(x) = a_t\psi_0(x) + b_t\psi_1(x)\) vanishes wherever the two terms cancel.

Wasserstein moves mass, and a flow that moves samples acts as a map \(T\), sending a position \(z\) to \(T(z)\). In one dimension the density it produces is \(\mu(T(z)) = \mu_0(z)\big/\tfrac{dT(z)}{dz}\), so from a start with no zeros the end can vanish only where \(dT(z)/dz = \infty\). The exact optimal map has exactly that at each zero of the end, which is why the exact geodesic has its zeros from the first instant. A flow with a smooth velocity field, which is what flow matching learns, cannot: it is a diffeomorphism at every time, so from a start with no zeros it only approaches the end, with dips that deepen as the network sharpens.

The outputs differ in the same way. A smooth \(\sqrt{\mu(x)}\) or \(\mu(x)\) can only touch zero, and a touching zero disappears under the smallest upward error. A real, signed \(\psi(x)\) passes through zero by changing sign, and a small error moves that zero but cannot remove it. (This needs the amplitude to be real: on a line a complex amplitude vanishes only where its real and imaginary parts vanish together, which a small complex error destroys.) Physics has long used networks that output signed or complex amplitudes directly, and flows that transport samples meet the same obstacle at sign changes.

None of this contradicts the previous section. Samples cannot tell \(\psi(x)\) from \(\lvert\psi(x)\rvert\), so choosing a signed root changes nothing the data can check. It changes what the network has to output. Fubini-Study therefore offers two things, which are worth keeping apart. Its geometry, the phase direction and the interference term, helps only when the data carry a sign or a phase. Its representation, a real amplitude allowed to change sign, can help whenever the densities have zeros. Whether an easier function to output also makes the flow easier to train is an empirical question, and the recipe does not answer it.

Which geometry for which data

One recipe, a rule plus a price, gives all four geometries: run on densities, it reproduces the ones flow matching already uses; run on amplitudes, it adds a phase that samples cannot see and a representation that keeps zeros. The four differ in what their distances respond to.

  • Wasserstein, when distances in the underlying space should matter. Bumps far apart are far apart, and the interpolant moves mass rather than teleporting it. Point clouds and spatial densities are examples.
  • Mixture, as the baseline. It is the simplest and is exact on the simplex, but its price ignores how much mass is already present, and its distance depends on the units of \(x\). Dirichlet Flow Matching reports that linear flow matching on the simplex behaves pathologically and was built to avoid it, and statistical-manifold flow matching benchmarks against it.
  • Fisher-Rao, when the categories are exchangeable. Its distance ignores where mass sits, does not depend on units, and saturates at \(\pi\) once the supports separate. Categorical data are the natural case.
  • Fubini-Study, when the sign or phase is part of the object: quantum states measured in several bases, STFT frames, MRI \(k\)-space, eigenfunctions that change sign. On samples of a density its geometry adds nothing, as above. Its representation is a separate matter: when the densities have zeros, a real signed amplitude is the smoother function to output.

The recipe has three slots, the state, the rule and the price, and each can be filled in other ways. Two examples:

  • Both rules at once. Allow transport and birth and death together and add their prices: the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao geometry of unbalanced optimal transport. A length scale set by the relative price decides when moving mass is cheaper than destroying and recreating it.
  • Jumps on a graph. If the categories have structure (ordinal labels, neighbouring tokens), let mass hop between neighbours and price the hops like kinetic energy: Maas’s discrete Wasserstein distance, under which nearby categories are close, unlike under Fisher-Rao. The rule is a continuous-time Markov chain, the kind of process discrete flow matching samples, though there its state is a token rather than a distribution.

The mixture, Fisher-Rao and Fubini-Study geometries have closed-form geodesics, exponential and logarithm maps: the first is flat, Fisher-Rao is a sphere, and Fubini-Study is a sphere with global phases identified. Wasserstein has them in one dimension and between Gaussians. In these cases the Riemannian objective applies as it stands. The recipe says what each geometry charges for, and so which path is cheapest. It does not say which path through that space is easiest for a network to learn, which remains an empirical question for each kind of data.
